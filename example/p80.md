# The five reasonings that establish selflessness — and the vajra-sliver applied to the sprout

## 1 · Passage & Context

**Source Text:**

> གཉིས་པ་ལ། སྤྱིར་བདག་མེད་གཏན་ལ་འབེབས་པའི་རིགས་པ་ལ་དུ་མ་ཡོད་དེ། ཆོས་རྣམས་ཀྱི་ངོ་བོ་ལ་དཔྱོད་པ་གཅིག་དུ་བྲལ་གྱི་གཏན་ཚིགས། རྒྱུ་ལ་དཔྱོད་པ་རྡོ་རྗེ་གཟེགས་མའི་གཏན་ཚིགས། འབྲས་བུ་ལ་དཔྱོད་པ་ཡོད་མེད་སྐྱེ་འགོག་གི་གཏན་ཚིགས༑ རྒྱུ་འབྲས་གཉིས་ཀ་ལ་དཔྱོད་པ་མུ་བཞི་སྐྱེ་འགོག་གི་གཏན་ཚིགས། རིགས་པའི་རྒྱལ་པོ་རྟེན་འབྲེལ་གྱི་གཏན་ཚིགས་རྣམས་སུ་ཡོད་པའི་ཕྱིར། དང་པོ་དེ་འོག་ཏུ་འཆད༑ གཉིས་པ་ནི། མྱུ་གུ་ཆོས་ཅན། དོན་དམ་པར་མི་སྐྱེ་སྟེ། དོན་དམ་པར་བདག་དང་། གཞན་དང་། གཉིས་ཀ་དང་། རྒྱུ་མེད་གང་རུང་ལས་མི་སྐྱེ་བའི་ཕྱིར་ཏེ། རྩ་བ་ཤེས་རབ་ལས༑ བདག་ལས་མ་ཡིན་གཞན་ལས་མིན། ། གཉིས་ལས་མ་ཡིན་རྒྱུ་མེད་མིན། ། དངོས་པོ་གང་དག་གང་ན་ཡང་། ། སྐྱེ་བ་ནམ་ཡང་ཡོད་མ་ཡིན། ། ཞེས་གསུངས་པའི་ཕྱིར།

**Preceding Section:** p79 closes the *previous* sub-topic, not this one. It is
still arguing about what "existent" means: what is posited by the force of
appearing to a mind is what conventional existence amounts to
(བློ་ལ་སྣང་བའི་དབང་གིས་བཞག་པའི་ཡོད་པ་དེ་ཀུན་རྫོབ་ཏུ་ཡོད་པའི་དོན), witnessed from
the དབུ་མ་སྣང་བ, the འཇུག་ཊཱིཀ and the བདེན་གཉིས. It ends with a list that matters
for reading p80 correctly: བདེན་པར་གྲུབ་པ, དོན་དམ་པར་གྲུབ་པ and
ཡང་དག་པར་གྲུབ་པ do not exist, while རང་གི་མཚན་ཉིད་ཀྱིས་གྲུབ་པ,
རང་ངོས་ནས་གྲུབ་པ, རང་བཞིན་གྱིས་གྲུབ་པ and རྫས་སུ་གྲུབ་པ do. This is a
Svātantrika-register discussion, and it is what licenses the qualifier
དོན་དམ་པར་ that every thesis in p80 carries.

**Following Section:** p81 continues the enumeration without a break — གསུམ་པ་ནི།
takes up the third reason (ཡོད་མེད་སྐྱེ་འགོག, the refutation of production of the
already-existent and the non-existent, with a verse from the དབུ་མ་སྣང་བ), and
བཞི་པ་ནི། the fourth (མུ་བཞི་སྐྱེ་འགོག, the four alternatives of one and many
causes and effects, with a verse from the བདེན་གཉིས). p80's own second half is
therefore the first instalment of a series that runs at least through p81.

**What this paragraph is doing.** Two distinct things, and running them together
is the main way to misread it. First it gives a *list*: the reasonings that
establish selflessness, five of them, each classified by what it examines.
Then — after setting the first aside for later — it *performs* the second one,
the vajra-sliver, on the standard example of the sprout, and seals it with
Nāgārjuna's opening verse.

**On the source of the verse.** རྩ་བ་ཤེས་རབ་ is the
*Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*, and the four lines are its **first verse**, standing at
the head of chapter 1 immediately after the eight-negation homage. In this
archive the root text itself is
`Tengyur/04 DBU MA_Middle Way School (Madhyamaka)/VOL 1 (TZA)/TD03824_DBU MA RTZA BA'I TSIG LE'UR BYAS PA SHES RAB CES BYA BA (Prajñā Nāma Mūla Madhyamaka Kārikā)`,
where [the verse](quote:p80-q01) begins at character **378** — the maṅgala
verses run to 377. It is worth saying which text this is: the same four lines are quoted by Tsongkhapa,
by Dharmamitra and by Chökyi Gyaltsen himself, all of them cited below, and it
would be easy to attribute the verse to whichever of them the search happened to
surface. The words are Nāgārjuna's.

**On the qualifier.** དོན་དམ་པར་ appears twice in the thesis and once inside the
reason, and it is doing the whole work. Without it the argument would deny
production altogether; with it, it denies only *ultimate* production, which is
precisely what p79 has just spent a paragraph making room for. A translation
that drops it in either place has changed the philosophical position.

**On the count.** The paragraph says སྤྱིར … དུ་མ་ཡོད་དེ, "in general there are
several", and then gives five. It does not say "there are five". The list is
presented as illustrative rather than exhaustive, which matters for §5 below.

## 3 · Structural Context (Dkar Chag)

- **Abhisamayālaṃkāra location:** *not* in the root verses, and the negative is
  worth stating plainly. The phrase this whole excursus hangs on —
  གཅིག་དང་དུ་མའི་ངོ་བོ་ཉིད་དང་བྲལ་བའི་ཕྱིར — was searched for in Maitreya's text
  and **is not there**. It belongs to the commentarial layer, not to the
  kārikās. So this paragraph has no direct counterpart in the Ornament, and any
  attempt to assign it one would be manufactured.
- **Haribhadra's commentary location:** it does have a counterpart in
  Haribhadra, very near the beginning of the འགྲེལ་པ་དོན་གསལ, where he says
  ground, path and aspects are known to be unproduced
  [གཅིག་དང་དུ་མའི་ངོ་བོ་ཉིད་དང་བྲལ་བའི་ཕྱིར་ཞེས་བྱ་བ་ལ་སོགས་པའི་ཚད་མས](quote:p80-q06)
  — "by the valid cognition of 'because it is free from being one or many', and
  so forth." Haribhadra names one reasoning and then writes *and so forth*. He
  does not enumerate. **This paragraph is unpacking his ལ་སོགས་པ.**
  That is the precise structural relation, and it explains why the passage feels
  like Madhyamaka rather than Prajñāpāramitā: it is filling a gap the Indian
  commentary left open by an abbreviation.
- **Text location:** within སྐབས་དང་པོའི་སྤྱི་དོན།, under the topic
  གཅིག་དུ་བྲལ། (opening at p65), inside རང་ལུགས་ལ། (from p78), as the second
  member: ༢༽ དེ་གཞི་གཞན་གྱི་སྟེང་དུ་འགོག་བྱེད་ཀྱི་རིགས་པ་བཤད་པའོ། — "explaining
  the reasonings that refute [true existence] on the basis of something else."
  p80 opens that section, which is why it begins with a bare གཉིས་པ་ལ།.
- **Section type:** own-system exposition (རང་ལུགས), not objection and reply.
  There is no ཁ་ཅིག and no opponent in the paragraph; it is a schema followed by
  a worked instance.
- **Argumentative role:** it converts a name into a method. The topic heading
  promised reasonings that refute true existence; the paragraph supplies the
  inventory and then demonstrates one of them in the standard formal shape
  (ཆོས་ཅན, thesis, reason, scriptural support), which is the form the next
  several paragraphs repeat.

## 4 · Philosophical Exposition & Commentary

**Core meaning.** The five reasonings are classified by their *object of
analysis*, and that is the organising idea: essence, cause, effect, cause and
effect together, and — standing apart — dependent origination. The first four
are analytic refutations; the fifth is the positive reason that is called the
king of reasonings because it establishes emptiness without relying on
refutation. The paragraph then applies the second to the sprout.

**Chökyi Gyaltsen's own treatment elsewhere, and what it adds.** The author sets
out the same material in his དབུ་མའི་སྤྱི་དོན, and the comparison is instructive
on two counts.

First, the *division there is two-tiered*. He opens by saying the reasonings
establishing the absence of true existence are of two sorts —
[ཐལ་འགྱུར་གྱི་དབང་དུ་བྱས་པ་དང་། གཏན་ཚིགས་ཀྱི་དབང་དུ་བྱས་པ་གཉིས།](quote:p80-q02)
— consequences and probative reasons, and only then, under the second heading,
gives [the list of five](quote:p80-q03) in wording that matches this paragraph
almost exactly. **Here in the Prajñāpāramitā textbook the upper tier is gone.**
p80 says simply "in general there are several reasonings that establish
selflessness" and goes straight to the five, which are the probative reasons
only. The four kinds of consequence — the *prasaṅga* that generates an inference
in the opponent, the parity-of-reason consequence, the contradiction-declaring
consequence, and the consequence that the proof is as hard to establish as the
thesis — are simply not mentioned. A reader who knows only p80 would take the
five for the whole field. They are half of it.

Second, he explains *why the vajra-sliver comes second and gets the worked
example*: [དེ་རྣམས་ཀྱི་ནང་ནས་འཕགས་པ་ཡབ་སྲས་ཀྱིས་གཙོ་བོར་རྡོ་རྗེ་གཟེགས་མའི་གཏན་ཚིགས་ཀྱི་སྒོ་ནས་བདེན་མེད་གཏན་ལ་འབེབས་པར་མཛད་པ་ཡིན་ཏེ།](quote:p80-q04)
— "of these, the noble father and son [Nāgārjuna and Āryadeva] principally
established the absence of true existence by way of the vajra-sliver reason."
The ordering in p80 is not arbitrary and not merely pedagogical: it follows what
the Indian founders themselves emphasised. The paragraph does not say this. The
same author's other textbook does, and it is the reason the paragraph looks the
way it does.

**The Indian precedent for the list.** Dharmamitra, commenting on Haribhadra's
ལ་སོགས་པ, spells out what the "and so forth" covers, and he begins exactly where
this paragraph does: [སོགས་པ་ཞེས་བྱ་བ་ནི་རྡོ་རྗེ་གཟེགས་མར།](quote:p80-q07),
"'and so forth' means: in the vajra-sliver, [the verse] 'not from self, not from
other…'" — and he then quotes the very verse p80 quotes, before going on to
dependent origination and to the refutation of production by many causes of one
effect. Three of p80's five are already there, in an Indian commentary, attached
to the same lemma. This is a stronger warrant than the paragraph itself claims,
and it is worth recording that the enumeration is not a Tibetan invention laid
over a silent Indian text; it is an expansion of something Dharmamitra had
already begun.

**Gyaltsab on the same lemma.** Gyaltsab poses the question this whole excursus
answers — by what valid cognition are ground, path and aspects established as
unproduced? — and answers with the same pair:
[གཅིག་དང་དུ་མའི་ངོ་བོ་ཉིད་དང་བྲལ་བའི་ཕྱིར་ཅེས་བྱ་བ་དང་རྡོ་རྗེ་གཟེགས་མའི་གཏན་ཚིགས་ལ་སོགས་པའི་རྟགས](quote:p80-q08).
He adds a curricular datum p80 omits: this establishment happens **first at the
path of accumulation** (དང་པོར་ཚོགས་ལམ་གྱི་གནས་སྐབས་སུ་གྲུབ་ཅིང་), and the
familiarisation with it is what yields the path of seeing and omniscience. That
places the reasonings on the path map, which is what a Prajñāpāramitā textbook
ultimately needs them for and which p80, taken alone, does not supply.

**Tsongkhapa reaching the same verse.** In the ལེགས་བཤད་གསེར་ཕྲེང Tsongkhapa
works through the four alternatives one at a time — production from self would
make cause and effect one and require perpetual re-production; production from
other would let everything arise from everything; from both incurs both faults;
without a cause, arising at every place and time — and only then closes with
[the verse](quote:p80-q10). The verse arrives as a seal on an argument already
made. p80 uses it the same way, but compresses the four refutations into the
single clause རྒྱུ་མེད་གང་རུང་ལས་མི་སྐྱེ་བའི་ཕྱིར་ཏེ. The compression is
appropriate to a spyi don and to a debating audience, who supply the four
refutations themselves; it does mean the paragraph asserts as a reason what
Tsongkhapa treats as a conclusion needing four separate arguments.

**The verse in its own text.** Reading MMK 1.1 where it stands rather than where
it is quoted changes what the paragraph is doing. In Nāgārjuna it is the opening
move of the treatise: the eight negations of the homage have just been stated,
and 1.1 launches the refutation of production, with the four conditions
(རྐྱེན་རྣམ་བཞི) following immediately in 1.2. The verse is a *programme*, not a
conclusion drawn from prior argument. When p80 attaches it to a formal thesis
about the sprout, it is borrowing the programmatic opening of the
*Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* as warrant for a particular inference — which is precisely
the move §5 examines.

**How far back the schema goes.** Ngok Lotsāwa, four centuries earlier and well
outside this curriculum, already classifies the reasonings by what each
examines: [ངོ་བོ་ལ་དཔྱོད་པ་གཅིག་དང་དུ་བྲལ་གྱི་གཏན་ཚིགས། རྒྱུ་ལ་དཔྱོད་པ་རྡོ་རྗེ་གཟེགས་མའི་གཏན་ཚིགས། འབྲས་བུ་ལ་དཔྱོད་པ་ཡོད་མེད་སྐྱེ་འགོག་གི་གཏན་ཚིགས་སོ། །](quote:p80-q09)
— essence, cause, effect. The classificatory principle is identical and the
wording nearly so, but **the list stops at three.** The fourth (both) and the
fifth (dependent origination) are later additions. This is a real finding about
the passage: p80 transmits a schema with a long pre-Gelug pedigree, in a form
that has grown by two members since Ngok. Where and by whom the two were added
is not something the evidence here settles, and I do not want to guess at it.

## 5 · Bones of Contention 🦴

**What is disputed?** Not the list. The sharp question is what the root verse is
being asked to do.

**Who argues what?** p80 states the thesis, gives the four-alternative
non-production as its reason, and then cites Nāgārjuna with the standard formula
ཞེས་གསུངས་པའི་ཕྱིར — "because it is said." Grammatically the citation is offered
as establishing the reason. But the same author, in the དབུ་མའི་སྤྱི་དོན, says of
this very verse:
[ཞེས་སོགས་ཀྱིས་ཀྱང་མཐའ་བཞི་ལས་སྐྱེ་བ་མེད་པའི་དམ་བཅའ་ཙམ་ཞིག་བསྟན་པ་ཡིན་གྱི་མཐའ་བཞི་ལས་མི་སྐྱེ་བ་རྟགས་སུ་བཀོད་ནས་དངོས་པོ་རྣམས་རང་བཞིན་གྱིས་སྐྱེ་བ་མེད་པར་སྒྲུབ་པའི་རྟགས་ཡང་དག་བསྟན་པ་མ་ཡིན་ཏེ།](quote:p80-q05)
— "by this and the like, only the *thesis* that there is no production from the
four extremes is taught; it does not set out non-production from the four
extremes as a reason and thereby present a valid probative reason establishing
that things are without inherent production." He grounds that on Nāgārjuna's own
auto-commentary.

**Why does it matter?** Because it decides what kind of thing a debater may do
with the verse. If the verse states a thesis, then citing it establishes that
Nāgārjuna holds the conclusion, and the *reason* still has to be argued — as
Tsongkhapa in fact argues it, alternative by alternative. If the verse supplies
the reason, the argument in p80 is complete as it stands. The two readings
license different moves on the debate ground, and a student who takes the
paragraph at face value will be caught out by an opponent who quotes the
author's own Madhyamaka textbook back at them.

**Resolution.** I do not think this is a contradiction, and I want to be careful
about how far the evidence takes me. The consistent reading is that the citation
formula here does what such formulae usually do in a spyi don — it supplies
scriptural warrant for the position, not a formalised probans — and that the
argumentative weight rests on the four refutations the paragraph leaves the
reader to supply, exactly as Tsongkhapa spells them out. On that reading the two
texts agree, and p80 is simply compressed. But the paragraph does not say so,
and the compression is precisely where the difficulty sits. **This is a point
where the Sera Jey position has to be reconstructed rather than read off**, and I
mark it as such: no text in the evidence available here states the harmonisation
explicitly.

A second, smaller contention: whether the enumeration is closed. p80 says
"in general there are several", Ngok has three, Dharmamitra names three under
Haribhadra's "and so forth", and the standard Gelug presentation has five. Anyone
treating five as a fixed doctrinal number is reading more into the paragraph than
it says.

## 6 · Key Terminology & Technical Concepts

- **རྩ་བ་ཤེས་རབ** — the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*.
  - **Sanskrit equivalent:** *Prajñā-nāma-mūlamadhyamakakārikā*, whose Tibetan
    title དབུ་མ་རྩ་བའི་ཚིག་ལེའུར་བྱས་པ་ཤེས་རབ་ the short form abbreviates.
  - **How understood here:** cited as the scriptural authority for
    non-production from the four extremes. Present in this archive as a Tengyur
    text in its own right (TD03824), so the citation can be checked against
    Nāgārjuna rather than against his Tibetan quoters.

- **རྡོ་རྗེ་གཟེགས་མའི་གཏན་ཚིགས** — the vajra-sliver reason.
  - **Sanskrit equivalent:** *vajrakaṇa-hetu*.
  - **How understood here:** the reason that investigates the *cause*, refuting
    production from self, other, both, or neither. Named for a sliver of vajra
    because it splits whatever it strikes. In this paragraph it is the second
    member and the one worked through.
  - **Debates:** whether Nāgārjuna's verse states it as a thesis or as a probans
    — see §5.

- **གཅིག་དུ་བྲལ་གྱི་གཏན་ཚིགས** — the reason of being free from one and many.
  - **How understood here:** the reason that investigates the *essence*. p80
    defers it (དང་པོ་དེ་འོག་ཏུ་འཆད), which is unusual: it is first in the list
    and last in treatment. The deferral is what the section heading
    གཅིག་དུ་བྲལ། at p65 has been building toward, so the paragraph is postponing
    its own topic's title reason in order to clear the others first.

- **ཡོད་མེད་སྐྱེ་འགོག** — the refutation of production of the existent and the
  non-existent.
  - **How understood here:** investigates the *effect*: an effect already
    existent at the time of its cause needs no production, and a non-existent
    one cannot be produced, like a sky-lotus. Taken up at p81.

- **མུ་བཞི་སྐྱེ་འགོག** — the refutation of production in the four alternatives.
  - **How understood here:** investigates *cause and effect together*: many
    causes producing one effect, many producing many, one producing many, one
    producing one. Note that these four are a different four from the vajra
    sliver's four, and the two are easily conflated; p81 keeps them apart.

- **རིགས་པའི་རྒྱལ་པོ་རྟེན་འབྲེལ་གྱི་གཏན་ཚིགས** — dependent origination, the king
  of reasonings.
  - **Sanskrit equivalent:** *pratītyasamutpāda*.
  - **How understood here:** it is the only member of the list that is not a
    refutation. It establishes the absence of inherent existence positively, from
    the fact of dependence, and it is called king because it establishes
    emptiness without falling to either extreme. Its position last in the list is
    a place of honour, not of weakness.

- **དོན་དམ་པར་** — "ultimately".
  - **How understood here:** the qualifier that makes the thesis defensible
    rather than nihilistic, and the register marker of the whole passage. See the
    note in §2.
