# The printer's dedication — a verse that is not Chökyi Gyaltsen's

## 1 · Passage & Context

**Source Text:**

> པར་བྱང་སྨོན་ཚིག རྣམ་གྲོལ་ཞི་བའི་རྒྱ་མཚོར་རོ་གཅིག་ཏུ། ། གཞོལ་བའི་མཐའ་ཡས་ཆོས་ཚུལ་དལ་འབབ་ཀླུང་། ། ཚིག་གི་གཟེགས་མ་རེས་ཀྱང་དུག་གསུམ་གྱི། ། དྲི་མ་ཡོངས་འཁྲུད་ལེགས་བཤད་བདུད་རྩི་འདི། ། དཔར་དུ་བསྐྲུན་པའི་རྣམ་དཀར་ཕུང་པོའི་མཐུས། ། ཡང་དག་ཆོས་ཀྱི་སྒོ་མོ་སྟོང་ཕྲག་བརྒྱ། ། གདངས་ཏེ་རྒྱུད་ལྔའི་འགྲོ་བ་མ་ལུས་པ། ། ངེས་ལེགས་ནོར་བུའི་མཛོད་ལ་དབང་འབྱོར་ཤོག །

**Preceding Section:** p424 is the last paragraph of the body of the text. It explains Haribhadra's gloss on the tenth and final instruction, the instruction on the path of meditation (སྒོམ་ལམ་ལ་གདམས་པ): the sūtra passage beginning རབ་འབྱོར་འདུས་བྱས་ཀྱི་དབྱིངས is an instruction on the path of meditation because it teaches that path as the antidote to the innate objects of abandonment. The paragraph then argues that the Mahāyāna path of meditation that directly realises the ultimate is not of a different type (རིགས་ཐ་དད) from the Mahāyāna path of seeing. It answers an objection: if the two are not different in type, they should have equal power to abandon. The reply is that only the path of meditation, through longer cultivation, can abandon the innate obscurations to omniscience, and that this follows from the nature of dependent arising. It closes by explaining why the instructions number exactly ten: practice itself, its object (the four truths), its support (the Three Jewels), and the causes that remove adverse conditions and supply favourable ones. It ends by quoting Haribhadra's summary, ending གདམས་ངག་ནི་རྣམ་པ་བཅུའོ, and then says ཞེས་པའི་གཞུང་འདི་རྣམས་གསུངས་སོ. That is the last sentence the author wrote in this volume.

**Following Section:** p426 is the verse's own prose colophon. It gives the verse's purpose: to spread the tradition of Śākyasiṃha, who teaches a non-violent path of peace across the world. It gives the verse's scope, [a dedication-prayer that may be attached to the printing, or the funding of the printing, of any Buddhist scripture of any tenet system](quote:p425-q02). And it gives the author, "the Śākya monk and Dharma-speaker Ngawang Lobsang Tenzin Gyatso". It ends with དགེའོ, and that is the end of the volume.

**What this paragraph is doing.** Nothing in the argument of the *Ornament*. This is a printing colophon (པར་བྱང), in the form of a dedication-prayer (སྨོན་ཚིག). The publisher of this modern edition added it after Chökyi Gyaltsen's text had ended. The heading glued to its first line, པར་བྱང་སྨོན་ཚིག, is the edition's label for it and not part of the verse. The same eight lines, with the same prose colophon, close almost every volume in this archive's modern editions. Among them are the Ornament and Haribhadra (pack), Tsongkhapa's *Golden Garland* (pack), the other volumes of Chökyi Gyaltsen's *yig cha*, a Collected Topics manual and Chökyi Gyaltsen's biography. The Vinaya general meaning heads it explicitly as [the printing-colophon prayer "spoken by Gongsa Chok"](quote:p425-q01). Gongsa Chok is the honorific for the Dalai Lama. The name signed in p426, Ngawang Lobsang Tenzin Gyatso, is the ordination name of the Fourteenth Dalai Lama. The attribution to him rests on those two facts together; neither is in the verse itself.

## 2 · Translation & Breakdown

<!--line 0-95-->
> **པར་བྱང་སྨོན་ཚིག རྣམ་གྲོལ་ཞི་བའི་རྒྱ་མཚོར་རོ་གཅིག་ཏུ། ། གཞོལ་བའི་མཐའ་ཡས་ཆོས་ཚུལ་དལ་འབབ་ཀླུང་། །**
>
> [Heading:] *Printing colophon: a dedication-prayer.* — The gently flowing river (དལ་འབབ་ཀླུང) of the limitless ways of Dharma (ཆོས་ཚུལ), which runs down (གཞོལ་བ) into the ocean of peaceful liberation (རྣམ་གྲོལ་ཞི་བ) to become there of one taste (རོ་གཅིག) —

<!--line 95-176-->
> **ཚིག་གི་གཟེགས་མ་རེས་ཀྱང་དུག་གསུམ་གྱི། ། དྲི་མ་ཡོངས་འཁྲུད་ལེགས་བཤད་བདུད་རྩི་འདི། །**
>
> this nectar of excellent explanation (ལེགས་བཤད་བདུད་རྩི), of which even the spray of its words (ཚིག་གི་གཟེགས་མ) washes the stains of the three poisons (དུག་གསུམ) completely away —

<!--line 176-257-->
> **དཔར་དུ་བསྐྲུན་པའི་རྣམ་དཀར་ཕུང་པོའི་མཐུས། ། ཡང་དག་ཆོས་ཀྱི་སྒོ་མོ་སྟོང་ཕྲག་བརྒྱ། །**
>
> by the power of the mass of white virtue (རྣམ་དཀར་ཕུང་པོ) in having had it printed (དཔར་དུ་བསྐྲུན་པ), may a hundred thousand doors of the authentic Dharma

<!--line 257-334-->
> **གདངས་ཏེ་རྒྱུད་ལྔའི་འགྲོ་བ་མ་ལུས་པ། ། ངེས་ལེགས་ནོར་བུའི་མཛོད་ལ་དབང་འབྱོར་ཤོག །**
>
> swing open, and may the beings of the five kinds of existence (རྒྱུད་ལྔའི་འགྲོ་བ), every one of them, come into possession of the treasury of the jewel of definite goodness (ངེས་ལེགས).

**Notes on the reading.**

- Syntax: the first four lines are one long noun phrase ending in འདི, "this nectar…". The rest of the sentence is carried by the printing clause (དཔར་དུ་བསྐྲུན་པའི་…མཐུས) and the two wishes, the doors opening (གདངས་ཏེ) and the beings coming into possession (དབང་འབྱོར་ཤོག). The "nectar of excellent explanation" is whatever book the verse has been attached to. The verse was written so that it would fit any book, which is why nothing in it names this one.
- ཚིག་གི་གཟེགས་མ་རེས་ཀྱང: གཟེགས་མ is a fine spray or scattering of droplets. I read རེས་ཀྱང as "even when it has touched," which fits the washing image. It could also be the adverbial "at times, even occasionally." Either way the sense is that even a little of the text purifies. I mark the construal as uncertain.
- རྒྱུད་ལྔའི་འགྲོ་བ: I take this as the beings of the five destinies (the five-*gati* scheme, with the demigods not counted separately), meaning all saṃsāric beings. It is not the five buddha families.
- Variant readings in the archive's other copies: the copies in the Vinaya general meaning and the chapter-two *mtha' dpyod* volume read དལ་འབབས for དལ་འབབ and པར་དུ for དཔར་དུ. Neither changes the meaning. In the following prose colophon, this volume reads རྣམ་འགྲེལ་ཤཱཀྱ་སེངྒེ, where the copies at the end of the modern Ornament, Haribhadra and the *Golden Garland* read རྣམ་འདྲེན་ཤཱཀྱ་སེངྒེ, "the Guide, Śākyasiṃha". རྣམ་འདྲེན is plainly the right reading.

## 3 · Structural Context (Dkar Chag)

- **Abhisamayālaṃkāra location:** none. The pack announces no lemma, and none can be argued, because the verse comments on nothing in the root text. I read the end of the canonical Ornament in the Tengyur. It closes with its colophon, [the work complete, translated by the paṇḍita Gomi Chimé and the translator Loden Sherab](quote:p425-q05), and nothing follows. The same eight lines do follow that colophon in the modern printed Ornament in `Commentaries/` (pack, passage 1), which shows that the verse travels with the printing and not with the text.
- **Haribhadra's commentary location:** none, and the absence can be checked. The canonical འགྲེལ་པ་དོན་གསལ། (Tengyur, Toh. 3793) ends with Haribhadra's own verses. The last of them before his colophon is [ཀྱེ་མ་གཞུང་ལུགས་སྣ་ཚོགས་ཀྱིས། །དུབ་པ་བདག་གིས་ངལ་གསོའི་གནས། །ཤེས་རབ་ཕ་རོལ་ཕྱིན་པའི་ལུགས། །རིང་མོ་ཞིག་ནས་མཐོང་བ་ཡིན།](quote:p425-q06), "wearied by many systems of doctrine, I have at last seen the way of the Perfection of Wisdom as my place of rest". After that come the author and translator colophons, and then the text ends. The printer's verse is not in the canonical text. It is appended after those colophons only in the modern printed edition in this archive (pack, passage 46). So the Indian commentary has nothing corresponding to this paragraph, and it could not have, since the verse was written for modern printings.
- **Text location:** the pack places it at the head of its own heading, པར་བྱང་སྨོན་ཚིག, which it nests under སྒོམ་ལམ་ལ་གདམས་པ།. That nesting is produced by how the paragraphs were segmented; Chökyi Gyaltsen did not put it there. The heading is the edition's label, and the verse stands outside the author's outline altogether. The author's text ends in p424. One further point: in this series a volume normally ends with the author's own chapter close (སྐབས་བསྡུ་བ). The fourth-chapter general meaning, for example, ends [གསུམ་པ་སྐབས་བསྡུ་བ་ནི། … ལེགས་བཤད་སྐལ་བཟང་ཀླུ་དབང་གི་རོལ་མཚོ་ཞེས་བྱ་བ་ལས་སྐབས་བཞི་པའི་ལེགས་པར་བཤད་པའོ།](quote:p425-q03). The first-chapter *mtha' dpyod* ends [… ལེགས་བཤད་གསེར་གྱི་ཕྲེང་བ་ཞེས་བྱ་བ་ལས། སྐབས་དང་པོའི་མཐའ་དཔྱོད་བཤད་ཟིན་ཏོ།](quote:p425-q04). This volume, as transcribed, has no such line. It goes from ཞེས་པའི་གཞུང་འདི་རྣམས་གསུངས་སོ straight to the printer's verse. The work's title, ལེགས་བཤད་སྐལ་བཟང་ཀླུ་དབང་གི་རོལ་མཚོ, appears in this volume only on its title line. I cannot tell from the digital text whether the printed book lacks a chapter close or the transcription dropped it. That question is **unclear**.
- **Section type:** back matter, specifically a publisher's printing colophon in verse (པར་བྱང་སྨོན་ཚིག).
- **Argumentative role:** none within the text. It dedicates the merit of printing the book.

## 4 · Philosophical Exposition & Commentary

**Core meaning.** The verse makes one simple move, the one every dedication makes. Printing a book of Dharma is a virtuous act. Its "mass of white virtue" is dedicated so that the Dharma opens its "hundred thousand doors" and all beings reach definite goodness. The first quatrain praises the book by images. It is a river that runs into the ocean of liberation. It is nectar that washes away the three poisons. The second quatrain turns the merit of printing it toward a goal.

**The ocean of one taste.** The opening image is an old one. Many teachings of different kinds flow into liberation and there become a single taste, as rivers of different water all become salt in the sea. My own search found it in two sūtras of the Kangyur. In the *Saptaśatikā Prajñāpāramitā*, the Buddha tells Mañjuśrī that [just as water drawn from any of the four sides of the ocean is of one taste, the taste of salt, so all the Dharma he has taught is of one taste — the taste of non-arising, of no entity, of freedom from desire, of liberation, of cessation](quote:p425-q07). In the *Saddharmapuṇḍarīka*, in the parable of the one rain that waters plants of every size, the Tathāgata knows [the Dharma as of one taste: the taste of liberation, culminating in nirvāṇa](quote:p425-q08). A commentary on the *Lotus* in the Tengyur (TD04017) glosses the sūtra's "one taste" in the same way, as the taste of signlessness, of the uncontaminated, and of liberation. I found it while searching and do not quote it here. I offer these passages as the canonical background of the image, not as sources the verse cites. The verse cites nothing. It is still fitting that the first of the two is a Prajñāpāramitā sūtra, bound here at the end of a Prajñāpāramitā textbook.

**Who holds this.** Only one voice is speaking, and it is not Chökyi Gyaltsen's. The Vinaya general meaning gives the verse a heading of authorship, [the printing-colophon prayer spoken by Gongsa Chok](quote:p425-q01). The prose colophon in p426 names the composer and gives the verse's purpose, as [a dedication-prayer for the printing of any scripture of any Buddhist tenet system](quote:p425-q02). That phrase, གྲུབ་མཐའ་རིས་མེད, "without partiality among tenet systems", matters for how the verse is read. The author says it was written to be used anywhere, and that is exactly how the archive shows it being used: at the end of an Indian root text, an Indian commentary, Tsongkhapa, Chökyi Gyaltsen, a Collected Topics manual and a biography.

**The layering of the back matter.** The companion volumes show how this verse relates to the dedications that are specific to a text. At the end of the first-chapter *mtha' dpyod*, the author's close is followed first by [a quatrain written for this tradition, praying that "the system of Chökyi Gyaltsen, who made clear the hard points of the Perfection-of-Wisdom instructions without error by way of scripture and reasoning", flourish for a hundred aeons together with its lineage-holders](quote:p425-q04). The generic printer's verse comes only after that. Other volumes have yet another layer, a publisher's verse naming the monastic press. Tsongkhapa's *Golden Garland* has one naming Tashi Lhunpo Chödra, and Chökyi Gyaltsen's biography has one naming Kumbum Jampa Ling. Each is followed by the same verse as p425. Our volume, as transcribed, has neither the tradition-specific quatrain nor a press verse, only the generic dedication.

**Sera Jey specificity.** There is none to find. The verse was not written for Sera Jey, for the Gelug, or for the Perfection-of-Wisdom curriculum. It says so about itself, and in this archive the same lines close an Indian root text and its Indian commentary as well as Gelug works. What is specific to this curriculum is only the book it happens to be bound into.

**The evidence is thin, and that is the finding.** None of the pack's tier-1 to tier-3 passages comment on the verse. They are all the same verse, copied again at the end of another volume. There is no commentarial engagement with this paragraph, because it is not a passage the commentarial literature has any reason to engage.

## 5 · Bones of Contention 🦴

This paragraph does not open ཁ་ཅིག … ཞེས་ཟེར་ན, argues nothing, and nobody disputes it philosophically. The honest answer to "what is disputed" is that no doctrinal dispute attaches to it. Three matters of fact can be got wrong, though, and they are worth stating plainly for a reader who meets the verse at the end of the textbook.

- **Whose words are these?** A reader who takes everything bound between the covers as the yig cha could attribute the verse to Jetsun Chökyi Gyaltsen, which would be an attribution error. Within this archive the evidence points one way. There is the heading [གོང་ས་མཆོག་གིས་གསུངས་པའི](quote:p425-q01), and there is the signature of Ngawang Lobsang Tenzin Gyatso in the verse's [own colophon](quote:p425-q02). The verse belongs to a modern printing and a modern author, not to the sixteenth-century author of the text. I have not found a date for the verse in the archive.
- **Where does Chökyi Gyaltsen's text end?** It ends at p424. Whether this volume ever had the author's formal chapter close, the kind that [the fourth-chapter volume has](quote:p425-q03), is unclear from the transcription (section 3). A reader should not treat the verse as filling that place.
- **Is the dedication sectarian?** No. It calls itself a verse for the scriptures of any tenet system (གྲུབ་མཐའ་རིས་མེད), and it is attached as readily to Maitreya and Haribhadra as to Tsongkhapa and Chökyi Gyaltsen. Reading it as a Gelug or a Sera Jey statement would be reading into it.

**Resolution.** Nothing needs resolving at the level of doctrine. The Sera Jey reader's practical position is to read p425 as the publisher's dedication, recite it as such, and treat p424 as the last word of the *yig cha* on the first chapter.

## 6 · Key Terminology & Technical Concepts

- **པར་བྱང (printing colophon):** the statement a publisher adds at the end of a printed edition, recording or blessing the act of printing. It is distinct from the མཛད་བྱང (author's colophon) and the འགྱུར་བྱང (translators' colophon). The canonical Ornament and Haribhadra have the latter two and no པར་བྱང (section 3).
  - **How understood here:** the heading tells the reader that everything below it is outside the author's text.
- **རྣམ་དཀར་ཕུང་པོ (mass of white [virtue]):** the merit generated by an act, here the act of having the book printed (དཔར་དུ་བསྐྲུན་པ), which is then dedicated.
  - **How understood here:** it is the subject of the dedication: the merit of printing, not of studying or composing.
- **རོ་གཅིག (one taste):** *ekarasa*. Things different in their sources become a single taste in their destination.
  - **Sanskrit equivalent:** *ekarasa*
  - **How understood here:** the many ways of Dharma (ཆོས་ཚུལ་མཐའ་ཡས) all end in the single taste of liberation. The image has canonical precedent in the ocean simile of the [*Saptaśatikā*](quote:p425-q07) and the "one taste of liberation" of the [*Lotus*](quote:p425-q08).
  - **Debates:** in tantric and Mahāmudrā contexts, རོ་གཅིག names a stage of yoga, the "one taste" of appearance and emptiness. Nothing in this verse invokes that technical sense, and it should not be read in.
- **ངེས་ལེགས (definite goodness):** *niḥśreyasa*, the traditional partner of མངོན་མཐོ (*abhyudaya*, high rebirth). Within Gelug usage it covers both liberation and omniscience.
  - **Sanskrit equivalent:** *niḥśreyasa*
  - **How understood here:** the dedication aims past high rebirth to the final goals, which is why it is a "treasury of the jewel" to be possessed and not a passing benefit. I searched this archive's Tengyur for the pairing with Nāgārjuna's *Ratnāvalī*, its classic locus, and did not locate the *Ratnāvalī* there. So I offer the gloss without a canonical quotation.
- **རྒྱུད་ལྔའི་འགྲོ་བ (beings of the five kinds):** all saṃsāric beings, counted under the five-destiny scheme.
  - **How understood here:** it is the scope of the wish. I construe the phrase as five destinies and not the five buddha families (section 2).
- **གྲུབ་མཐའ་རིས་མེད (without partiality among tenet systems):** from the verse's own colophon (p426). It is not a technical term of Perfection-of-Wisdom exegesis, but it states the verse's scope and is the reason the verse carries no sectarian position.
