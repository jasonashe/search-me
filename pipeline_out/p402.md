# Closing the refuge excursus, opening "non-attachment" — Tsongkhapa's refuge verse and the sūtra of the objectifying bodhisattva

## 1 · Passage & Context

**Source Text:**

> གཉིས་པ་ནི། དཀོན་མཆོག་གསུམ་ལ་རང་གི་སྲོག་ལས་ཀྱང་གཅེས་པར་བཟུང་བའི་སྒོ་ནས་རྒྱུན་ལྡན་དུ་དྲན་པ་དང་། ཕྱག་དང་མཆོད་པ་ལ་སོགས་པ་གུས་པར་བྱ་ཞིང་། མ་དད་པའི་སེམས་སྐད་ཅིག་ཙམ་ཡང་མི་འབྱུང་བ་ལ་བསླབ་པར་བྱ་སྟེ། དེ་ལྟར་བྱས་པས་བདེ་ལེགས་ཐམས་ཅད་ཀྱི་གཞི་ཚུགས་པར་འགྱུར་བའི་ཕྱིར་ཏེ། རྗེ་བདག་ཉིད་ཆེན་པོས་ཀྱང་། སྐྱེ་དགུ་འཇིགས་རུང་འཁོར་བའི་གཡང་ས་རུ། ། གྲོགས་མེད་གཅིག་པུ་ཀུན་ཏུ་རྒྱུ་བ་ན། ། དཀོན་མཆོག་གསུམ་དང་འདྲ་བའི་སྐྱབས་གནས་མེད། ། དེ་ཕྱིར་བསམ་པ་ཐག་པས་སྐྱབས་སུ་སོང་། ། ཞེས་གསུངས་པའི་ཕྱིར། འོ་ན། མ་ཞེན་ཞེས་པས་བྱ་བ་ངན་ཞེན་གྱི་ལེ་ལོ་སྤོང་བྱེད་མ་ཞེན་པའི་བརྩོན་འགྲུས་ལ་གདམས་པ་དེ་འཆད་ན། དེའི་ཚུལ་གང་ཞེ་ན། འདི་ལ་མདོ་ལས་གསུངས་ཚུལ་དང་། བསྟན་བཅོས་ཀྱིས་འགྲེལ་ཚུལ་གཉིས་ལས། དང་པོ་ནི། དགེ་འདུན་དཀོན་མཆོག་ལ་གདམས་པའི་སྐབས་སུ། བྱང་ཆེན་ཁ་ན་མ་ཐོ་བ་དང་བཅས་པའི་ལུས་དང་ངག་དང་ཡིད་ཀྱི་ལས་རྣམས་ཀྱི་གོ་སྐབས་མི་དབྱེའོ། ། ཞེས་གསུངས་པ་ལས་འཕྲོས་ནས༑ དེ་སྐད་ཅེས་བཀའ་སྩལ་པ་དང་། ཚེ་དང་ལྡན་པ་ཤཱ་རི་དྭ་ཏིའི་བུས་བཅོམ་ལྡན་འདས་ལ་འདི་སྐད་ཅེས་གསོལ་ཏོ། ། བཅོམ་ལྡན་འདས་བྱང་ཆེན་ལུས་ཀྱི་ཁ་ན་མ་ཐོ་བ་དང་བཅས་པ་གང་ལགས། དེ་སྐད་ཅེས་གསོལ་པ་དང་། བཅོམ་ལྡན་འདས་ཀྱིས་ཚེ་དང་ལྡན་པ་ཤཱ་རི་དྭ་ཏིའི་བུ་ལ་འདི་སྐད་ཅེས་བཀའ་སྩལ་ཏོ། ། ཤཱ་རི་དྭ་ཏིའི་བུ། འདི་ལ་བྱང་ཆེན་འདི་སྙམ་དུ་གང་གིས་ལུས་ཀྱི་ལས་ཡང་དག་པར་བརྩོན་པའི་ལུས་དེ་ནི་གང་ཡིན། གང་གིས་ངག་གི་ལས་གང་གིས་ཡིད་ཀྱི་ལས་ཡང་དག་པར་བརྩོན་པའི་ཡིད་དེ་ནི་གང་ཡིན་སྙམ་པ་འདི་ནི། ཤཱ་རི་དྭ་ཏིའི་བུ། བྱང་ཆེན་ལུས་དང་ངག་དང་ཡིད་ཀྱི་ལས་བརྩོན་པ་ཁ་ན་མ་ཐོ་བ་དང་བཅས་པའོ། ། ཞེས་པ་ནས་ཤཱ་རིའི་བུ་བྱང་སེམས་ལམ་འདི་ལ་འགྲོ་ཞིང་ཕ་རོལ་ཏུ་ཕྱིན་པ་དྲུག་པོ་རྣམས་ལ་སྤྱོད་པ་ནི་ཐུབ་པར་དཀའ་བ་ཡིན་པའི་ཕྱིར་རོ། ། ཞེས་པའི་བར་གྱིས་བསྟན།

**Preceding Section:** p401 is the body of Chökyi Gyaltsen's refuge excursus, set inside his commentary on the Ornament's instruction on the Three Jewels. It runs through a fixed list: the objects of refuge have already been established; then the **cause** of going for refuge (fear of one's own suffering together with the conviction that the Three Jewels can protect from it); the **nature** of refuge (turning one's mind to them from the heart and committing to them), proved with the Bodhicaryāvatāra's བདག་ནི་འཇིགས་པས་རྣམ་སྐྲག་ནས། ། ཀུན་ཏུ་བཟང་ལ་བདག་ཉིད་འབུལ།; its divisions (body/speech/mind; small, middling and great beings; causal and resultant refuge; refuge arising from conventions and refuge gained by the force of reality); its **benefits**, with the verse སྐྱབས་སོང་བསོད་ནམས་གཟུགས་མཆིས་ན།; and finally the fifth heading, the **trainings**, which it divides as [the specific and the common trainings, the specific being again prohibitive and positive](quote:p402-q05). p401 gives only the specific ones: the prohibitive trainings from the Nirvāṇa-sūtra verses (having gone for refuge in the Dharma, give up harming; having gone for refuge in the Saṅgha, do not keep company with non-Buddhists), and the positive training from the Suhṛllekha verse on honouring even a wooden image of the Sugata.

**Following Section:** p403 is the second limb of the division this paragraph opens — the treatise's explanation (བསྟན་བཅོས་ཀྱིས་འགྲེལ་ཚུལ). It gives the lemma མ་ཞེན།, quotes Haribhadra's gloss on it (reading བསོད་ཉམས), and then turns the sūtra just quoted into a syllogism: the sūtra "Śāriputra, here a great bodhisattva thinks…" is an instruction in the exertion that does not cling to bad activity of the three doors, because it is a sūtra-statement instructing that body, speech and so on are equally without true existence, in order to abandon the laziness that clings to the pleasure (བདེ་བ) arising from the well-being of body, speech and mind. It then opens the next instruction, ཡོངས་སུ་མི་ངལ, in exactly the same frame (sūtra first, treatise second).

**What this paragraph is doing.** It is a seam. Its first half finishes the refuge excursus by giving the *common* trainings — keep the Three Jewels in mind as dearer than life, honour them, never let a moment of disbelief arise — and seals them with a verse of Tsongkhapa's. Its second half turns to the next item in the Ornament's list of ten instructions, མ་ཞེན "non-attachment", labels it the exertion that abandons the laziness of attachment to bad activity, and supplies the sūtra passage the line comments on, which it locates by the landmark in Haribhadra's rearranged Pañcaviṃśati where the instruction on the Saṅgha Jewel ends.

## 2 · Translation & Breakdown

<!--line 0-11-->
> **གཉིས་པ་ནི།**
>
> The second [the common trainings of refuge]:

<!--line 11-94-->
> **དཀོན་མཆོག་གསུམ་ལ་རང་གི་སྲོག་ལས་ཀྱང་གཅེས་པར་བཟུང་བའི་སྒོ་ནས་རྒྱུན་ལྡན་དུ་དྲན་པ་དང་།**
>
> by holding the Three Jewels (དཀོན་མཆོག་གསུམ) dearer even than one's own life, recollecting them continually;

<!--line 94-133-->
> **ཕྱག་དང་མཆོད་པ་ལ་སོགས་པ་གུས་པར་བྱ་ཞིང་།**
>
> making prostrations, offerings and the like with respect;

<!--line 133-190-->
> **མ་དད་པའི་སེམས་སྐད་ཅིག་ཙམ་ཡང་མི་འབྱུང་བ་ལ་བསླབ་པར་བྱ་སྟེ།**
>
> and training so that a mind of disbelief (མ་དད་པའི་སེམས) does not arise for even an instant —

<!--line 190-255-->
> **དེ་ལྟར་བྱས་པས་བདེ་ལེགས་ཐམས་ཅད་ཀྱི་གཞི་ཚུགས་པར་འགྱུར་བའི་ཕྱིར་ཏེ།**
>
> because by doing so the foundation of all well-being and excellence (བདེ་ལེགས) becomes established.

<!--line 255-281-->
> **རྗེ་བདག་ཉིད་ཆེན་པོས་ཀྱང་།**
>
> As the great-souled Lord [Tsongkhapa] too said:

<!--line 281-438-->
> **སྐྱེ་དགུ་འཇིགས་རུང་འཁོར་བའི་གཡང་ས་རུ། ། གྲོགས་མེད་གཅིག་པུ་ཀུན་ཏུ་རྒྱུ་བ་ན། ། དཀོན་མཆོག་གསུམ་དང་འདྲ་བའི་སྐྱབས་གནས་མེད། ། དེ་ཕྱིར་བསམ་པ་ཐག་པས་སྐྱབས་སུ་སོང་། །**
>
> "When beings roam everywhere, alone and without companion, on the terrifying precipice of saṃsāra, there is no place of refuge (སྐྱབས་གནས) like the Three Jewels; therefore go for refuge from the depths of the heart (བསམ་པ་ཐག་པས)."

<!--line 438-458-->
> **ཞེས་གསུངས་པའི་ཕྱིར།**
>
> — because he said that.

<!--line 458-568-->
> **འོ་ན། མ་ཞེན་ཞེས་པས་བྱ་བ་ངན་ཞེན་གྱི་ལེ་ལོ་སྤོང་བྱེད་མ་ཞེན་པའི་བརྩོན་འགྲུས་ལ་གདམས་པ་དེ་འཆད་ན། དེའི་ཚུལ་གང་ཞེ་ན།**
>
> Well then, if one is to explain how the words "non-attachment" (མ་ཞེན) instruct in the exertion of non-attachment (མ་ཞེན་པའི་བརྩོན་འགྲུས), which abandons the laziness of attachment to bad activity (བྱ་བ་ངན་ཞེན་གྱི་ལེ་ལོ) — how is it?

<!--line 568-640-->
> **འདི་ལ་མདོ་ལས་གསུངས་ཚུལ་དང་། བསྟན་བཅོས་ཀྱིས་འགྲེལ་ཚུལ་གཉིས་ལས། དང་པོ་ནི།**
>
> This has two parts, how it is spoken in the sūtra and how the treatise explains it. The first:

<!--line 640-763-->
> **དགེ་འདུན་དཀོན་མཆོག་ལ་གདམས་པའི་སྐབས་སུ། བྱང་ཆེན་ཁ་ན་མ་ཐོ་བ་དང་བཅས་པའི་ལུས་དང་ངག་དང་ཡིད་ཀྱི་ལས་རྣམས་ཀྱི་གོ་སྐབས་མི་དབྱེའོ། །**
>
> In the context of the instruction on the Saṅgha Jewel [the sūtra] says, "A great bodhisattva (བྱང་ཆེན) gives no opening to blameworthy (ཁ་ན་མ་ཐོ་བ་དང་བཅས་པ) actions of body, speech and mind."

<!--line 763-814-->
> **ཞེས་གསུངས་པ་ལས་འཕྲོས་ནས༑ དེ་སྐད་ཅེས་བཀའ་སྩལ་པ་དང་།**
>
> Following on from that statement: "When this had been said,

<!--line 814-882-->
> **ཚེ་དང་ལྡན་པ་ཤཱ་རི་དྭ་ཏིའི་བུས་བཅོམ་ལྡན་འདས་ལ་འདི་སྐད་ཅེས་གསོལ་ཏོ། །**
>
> the venerable Śāradvatīputra said this to the Bhagavān:

<!--line 882-939-->
> **བཅོམ་ལྡན་འདས་བྱང་ཆེན་ལུས་ཀྱི་ཁ་ན་མ་ཐོ་བ་དང་བཅས་པ་གང་ལགས།**
>
> 'Bhagavān, what is a great bodhisattva's blameworthy [action] of body?'

<!--line 939-1037-->
> **དེ་སྐད་ཅེས་གསོལ་པ་དང་། བཅོམ་ལྡན་འདས་ཀྱིས་ཚེ་དང་ལྡན་པ་ཤཱ་རི་དྭ་ཏིའི་བུ་ལ་འདི་སྐད་ཅེས་བཀའ་སྩལ་ཏོ། །**
>
> When he had asked this, the Bhagavān said to the venerable Śāradvatīputra:

<!--line 1037-1135-->
> **ཤཱ་རི་དྭ་ཏིའི་བུ། འདི་ལ་བྱང་ཆེན་འདི་སྙམ་དུ་གང་གིས་ལུས་ཀྱི་ལས་ཡང་དག་པར་བརྩོན་པའི་ལུས་དེ་ནི་གང་ཡིན།**
>
> 'Śāradvatīputra, here a great bodhisattva thinks: "What is that body by which one properly exerts oneself in bodily action?

<!--line 1135-1219-->
> **གང་གིས་ངག་གི་ལས་གང་གིས་ཡིད་ཀྱི་ལས་ཡང་དག་པར་བརྩོན་པའི་ཡིད་དེ་ནི་གང་ཡིན་སྙམ་པ་འདི་ནི།**
>
> By which [speech] verbal action, [and] what is that mind by which one properly exerts oneself in mental action?" — this thought,

<!--line 1219-1302-->
> **ཤཱ་རི་དྭ་ཏིའི་བུ། བྱང་ཆེན་ལུས་དང་ངག་དང་ཡིད་ཀྱི་ལས་བརྩོན་པ་ཁ་ན་མ་ཐོ་བ་དང་བཅས་པའོ། །**
>
> Śāradvatīputra, is the great bodhisattva's blameworthy exertion in actions of body, speech and mind' —

<!--line 1302-1444-->
> **ཞེས་པ་ནས་ཤཱ་རིའི་བུ་བྱང་སེམས་ལམ་འདི་ལ་འགྲོ་ཞིང་ཕ་རོལ་ཏུ་ཕྱིན་པ་དྲུག་པོ་རྣམས་ལ་སྤྱོད་པ་ནི་ཐུབ་པར་དཀའ་བ་ཡིན་པའི་ཕྱིར་རོ། ། ཞེས་པའི་བར་གྱིས་བསྟན།**
>
> is taught by the passage from there up to: 'Śāriputra, because a bodhisattva who travels on this path and practises the six perfections is hard to overcome (ཐུབ་པར་དཀའ་བ).'"

**On the abridgement at 1135–1219.** The quotation compresses the sūtra. Where [Haribhadra's edition](quote:p402-q09) has three parallel questions — the body, the speech and the mind "by which one properly undertakes" each kind of action — the yig cha keeps the body clause whole, reduces speech to a fragment (གང་གིས་ངག་གི་ལས), and completes only the mind clause. The translation above follows the Tibetan as it stands; the sense is plainly the three-part question.

**Variant readings in the sūtra quotation.** The yig cha reads ཡང་དག་པར་བརྩོན་པ and ལས་བརྩོན་པ where Haribhadra's edition reads ཡང་དག་པར་རྩོམ་པ and ལས་རྩོམ་པ ("undertake"); and it ends with ཐུབ་པར་དཀའ་བ ("hard to overcome") where [Haribhadra's edition](quote:p402-q11) reads ཐུབ་པར་མི་ནུས་པ ("cannot be overcome"). The yig cha itself uses ཐུབ་པར་མི་ནུས་པ when it quotes this sentence again to open the next instruction, ཡོངས་སུ་མི་ངལ. The variants make no difference to the argument. The reading བརྩོན does fit the heading (the whole subject is བརྩོན་འགྲུས), but nothing here shows whether that was a deliberate choice.

**The verse.** The paragraph quotes it with the readings གཅིག་པུ་ཀུན་ཏུ་རྒྱུ་བ and སྐྱབས་གནས་མེད. Tsongkhapa's collected works read [གཅིག་པུར་ཀུན་ཏ་འཁྱམས་པ and སྐྱབས་གཞན་མེད](quote:p402-q01). See §3 and §5.

## 3 · Structural Context (Dkar Chag)

- **Abhisamayālaṃkāra location:** the verse on the ten instructions (གདམས་ངག་བཅུ) in chapter one. The paragraph's first half belongs to the Ornament's second line, སངས་རྒྱས་ལ་སོགས་དཀོན་མཆོག་གསུམ།, and its second half to the first item of the next line: [སངས་རྒྱས་ལ་སོགས་དཀོན་མཆོག་གསུམ། ། མ་ཞེན་ཡོངས་སུ་མི་ངལ་དང་། ། ལམ་ནི་ཡོངས་སུ་འཛིན་པ་དང་། །](quote:p402-q06) The pack locates the lemma of the enclosing section at character 2998 of the root text, and I read it there.
- **Haribhadra's commentary location:** the འགྲེལ་པ་དོན་གསལ། quotes the whole verse at character 17507 and then glosses its items in one long sentence. For the Three Jewels it gives only the "transmission" (ལུང་འབོགས་པ) that each Jewel is to be approached without apprehending any object. It has **nothing** corresponding to the first half of this paragraph: no cause, nature, divisions, benefits or trainings of refuge, and no Tsongkhapa-style exhortation. The whole refuge excursus of p401–p402 is a Tibetan addition hung on this line. On མ་ཞེན, Haribhadra's entire gloss is [one clause](quote:p402-q07): one who has taken up exertion and works at the goal just described may come to cling to the བསོད་ནམས of body and the rest, so for non-attachment he teaches that body and the rest lack own-nature. **Haribhadra never uses the word ལེ་ལོ**, and he does not link the three instructions to three kinds of laziness. The label this paragraph gives the instruction (the exertion that abandons བྱ་བ་ངན་ཞེན་གྱི་ལེ་ལོ) comes from Tibetan commentary, not from the Indian one. I also read Tsongkhapa's ལེགས་བཤད་གསེར་ཕྲེང at this place (around character 313300). It goes straight from ལུང་འབོག་པ་གསུམ་ནི་དཀོན་མཆོག་གསུམ་ལ་འདོམས་པའོ to the fourth heading, with no refuge excursus either.
- **Text location:** from the pack's outline path: སྐྱབས་གསུམ་ལ་གདམས་པ།སངས་རྒྱས་ལ་སོགས་དཀོན་མཆོག་གསུམ། ། ཞེས་སོགས་ཀྱི་སྐབས་སུ་གཉིས། (opens at p376) → མ་ཞེན། ཞེས་སོགས་ཀྱི་སྐབས་སུ་གཉིས། (opens here at p402) → ༡༽ མདོ་ལས་གསུངས་ཚུལ་དང་། (also opens here). So the paragraph straddles a section boundary. Its first 458 characters are the last sub-part of the Three Jewels section (the second, common part of the fifth heading, the trainings). From the འོ་ན onwards it is the first limb of the new མ་ཞེན section. Chökyi Gyaltsen's own earlier outline of the ten instructions places མ་ཞེན under the fourth of its headings, "instruction on the cause of perfect practice": in the first of that heading's four parts, "abandoning the adverse side of practice", it is the first of three exertions (see [his outline](quote:p402-q16)).
- **Section type:** the first half is exposition, the trainings of the own system, closed with a scriptural seal (ལུང). The second half is the preliminary step of a standard two-part exposition, *how the sūtra says it* and *how the treatise explains it*. The same template recurs throughout Chökyi Gyaltsen's སྤྱི་དོན (among the pack's own leads it appears in his སྤྱི་དོན on chapters two, four and five and on the path of preparation), and here it supplies only the sūtra passage. There is no debate in this paragraph.
- **Argumentative role:** the second half fixes *which* sūtra text the Ornament's two syllables མ་ཞེན summarise. It does this by pointing to the join in Haribhadra's rearranged Pañcaviṃśati where the Saṅgha instruction ends, so the reader knows the lemma is anchored in the sūtra before p403 unpacks it. The first half does no argumentative work towards the Prajñāpāramitā topic. It completes a devotional and ethical presentation that the yig cha inserts at the Three Jewels.

## 4 · Philosophical Exposition & Commentary

**Core meaning — the first half.** The common trainings are stated in three clauses of rising inwardness: memory (recollect the Jewels constantly, holding them dearer than life), outward conduct (prostration, offering, respect), and the mind itself (not a moment of disbelief). The reason is not an argument but a foundation-claim: this is what establishes the basis of all well-being. The seal is a verse that the paragraph assigns to རྗེ་བདག་ཉིད་ཆེན་པོ. Searching Tsongkhapa's own collected works finds it: it is the refuge stanza of a short verse exhortation in volume ཁ, [སྐྱེ་དགུ་འཇིགས་རུང་འཁོར་བའི་གཡང་ས་རུ། །གྲོགས་མེད་གཅིག་པུར་ཀུན་ཏ་འཁྱམས་པ་ན། །དཀོན་མཆོག་གསུམ་དང་འདྲ་བའི་སྐྱབས་གཞན་མེད། །དེ་ཕྱིར་བསམ་པ་ཐག་པས་སྐྱབས་སུ་སོང༌། །](quote:p402-q01). Its colophon settles the author: [ཤཀྱའི་དགེ་སློང་བློ་བཟང་གྲགས་པའི་དཔལ་གྱིས་ལྕང་རིང་ཐང་ད་སྦྱར་བའོ།](quote:p402-q02), composed at the request of Drogön Dorje Pal. So "the great-souled Lord" is Tsongkhapa. The verse's own setting is worth knowing. In that little work it follows stanzas on the precious human rebirth, on death, and on karma following one "like a shadow", and it is followed by bodhicitta and the keeping of vows. It is a lam rim in miniature, and Chökyi Gyaltsen quotes it for the step at which fear of saṃsāra turns into refuge — the same "cause" of refuge that p401 had just defined.

**A twin passage.** A search for the verse found a second Abhisamayālaṃkāra commentary that has almost this whole half-paragraph: Kunkhyen Chöjor Pelsang's བསམ་འཕེལ་དབང་གི་རྒྱལ་པོ (in the Sungbum collection, a commentary on the Ornament together with Haribhadra and a subcommentary). It gives the same heading and the same three trainings ([དཀོན་མཆོག་གསུམ་རང་གི་སྲོག་ལས་ཀྱང་གཅེས་པར་གཟུངས་པའི་སྒོ་ནས་རྒྱུན་ལྡན་དུ་དྲན་པ་དང།…](quote:p402-q03)), the same reason, and the same verse. It differs in three details: "not even in a dream" (རྨི་ལམ་ཙམ་དུ་ཡང) where the yig cha has "not even for an instant"; Tsongkhapa's reading སྐྱབས་གཞན་མེད; and an attribution to [བརྩེ་བ་ཆེན་པོ](quote:p402-q04) rather than to རྗེ་བདག་ཉིད་ཆེན་པོ. In both commentaries the passage sits at the same place, the refuge excursus under the Three Jewels instruction. Chöjor Pelsang then passes to a ཐེག་ཆེན་དགྲ་བཅོམ་པ objection. Which text depends on which cannot be settled from what is available here: the archive does not date Chöjor Pelsang, and I have not established it. The parallel still matters. It shows the refuge excursus was a shared piece of Tibetan commentarial furniture at this line, not Chökyi Gyaltsen's private insertion.

**Core meaning — the second half: which sūtra, and in which edition.** The pack traces the quoted sūtra to the Pañcaviṃśatisāhasrikā. Reading the witnesses shows more exactly *which* Pañcaviṃśati Chökyi Gyaltsen is quoting. In the Kangyur's own twenty-five-thousand-line sūtra the Buddha's answer is worded quite differently: [བྱང་ཆུབ་སེམས་དཔའ་སེམས་དཔའ་ཆེན་པོ་འདི་སྙམ་དུ་སེམས་ཤིང། འདི་ནི་ལུས་ཡིན་གྱི་དེས་ནི་ལུས་ཀྱི་ལས་ཤིག་བརྩམ།](quote:p402-q10), "this is the body; by it let me undertake a bodily action". The Kangyur text also lacks the second "when he had asked this" formula. The yig cha's wording follows instead Haribhadra's eight-chapter rearrangement of the Pañcaviṃśati, the ལེའུ་བརྒྱད་མ preserved in the Tengyur, sentence for sentence: [ཤཱ་རིའི་བུ་འདི་ལ་བྱང་ཆུབ་སེམས་དཔའ་སེམས་དཔའ་ཆེན་པོ་འདི་སྙམ་དུ་གང་གི་ལུས་ཀྱི་ལས་ཡང་དག་པར་རྩོམ་པའི་ལུས་དེ་ནི་གང་ཡིན།…](quote:p402-q09). That edition also explains the paragraph's odd locating phrase "in the context of the instruction on the Saṅgha Jewel". Haribhadra wrote his section-markers into the sūtra itself, and the sentence the yig cha starts from is followed there by [དགེ་འདུན་དཀོན་མཆོག་ལ་གདམས་པའོ](quote:p402-q08). The end-point is marked the same way: [ཐུབ་པར་མི་ནུས་པ་ཡིན་ནོ་ཞེས་བྱ་བ་ནི་མ་ཞེན་པ་ལ་གདམས་པའོ](quote:p402-q11). So the sūtra words belong to the Pañcaviṃśati. The segmentation that lets the yig cha say where the instruction on མ་ཞེན begins and ends, and the exact wording quoted, are Haribhadra's. This is the text the Sera Jey curriculum treats as "the sūtra" throughout this chapter.

**What the sūtra is actually saying.** Read quickly, "a bodhisattva gives no opening to blameworthy actions of body, speech and mind" sounds like ordinary moral restraint. The Buddha's answer shows it is not. The blameworthy thing is the *thought* "what is the body by which I exert myself in bodily action?" — reifying the instrument and the agent of one's own virtuous effort. Daṃṣṭrasena's Śatasāhasrikā commentary, an Indian source at some remove from this yig cha, makes this explicit: [ཁ་ན་མ་ཐོ་བ་དང་བཅས་པའི་ལུས་དང་། ངག་དང་ཡིད་ཀྱི་ལས་ཞེས་བྱ་བ་ནི་སྐབས་འདིར་སྡིག་པ་མི་དགེ་བའི་ལས་ལ་མི་བྱའི། དམིགས་པ་དང་བཅས་པའི་ལུས་དང་ངག་ཡིད་གསུམ་གྱི་ལས་ཁ་ན་མ་ཐོ་བ་ཞེས་བྱ་སྟེ།](quote:p402-q12) — "here 'blameworthy' does not mean unwholesome, sinful action; objectifying (དམིགས་པ་དང་བཅས་པ) action of body, speech and mind is what is called blameworthy." This is what connects the sūtra to Haribhadra's gloss. The danger addressed is clinging (ཞེན) to one's own practising body, speech and mind and to their well-being, and the remedy is to see them as without own-nature. That is exactly what p403 will draw from this sūtra.

**Where "laziness" comes from.** The paragraph's label for the instruction (the exertion that abandons the laziness of attachment to bad activity) is not in the sūtra and not in Haribhadra. It comes from reading the three exertions of the Ornament's line through Śāntideva's list of what opposes exertion: [ལེ་ལོ་ངན་ལ་ཞེན་པ་དང། ། སྐྱིད་ལུག་བདག་ཉིད་བརྙས་པའོ། །](quote:p402-q15) (Bodhicaryāvatāra VII, cited from the Tengyur). Tsongkhapa makes the mapping explicit in the ལེགས་བཤད་གསེར་ཕྲེང: exertion is the chief thing in practice, its adverse side is threefold as Śāntideva says, and [the first, which abandons སྙོམས་ལས་འཛིན་པ, is the instruction on the exertion of non-attachment](quote:p402-q13). Chökyi Gyaltsen's own outline of the ten instructions, earlier in this same text, repeats Tsongkhapa's wording exactly: [སྙོམས་ལས་འཛིན་པའི་ལེ་ལོ་སྤོང་བྱེད་མ་ཞེན་པའི་བརྩོན་འགྲུས་ལ་གདམས་པ](quote:p402-q16). Then non-weariness (ཡོངས་སུ་མི་ངལ) answers discouragement (སྒྱིད་ལུག), and grasping the path answers self-contempt.

**Gyaltsab Je** does not use the three-laziness frame at this point. His outline makes the instruction "the cause of practice not declining", and his gloss is: [བྱ་བ་ངན་པ་ལ་མ་ཞེན་པ་ལ་འདོམས་པ་ཡིན་ཏེ། ལུས་ལ་སོགས་པ་དོན་དམ་པར་ངོ་བོ་ཉིད་མེད་པར་རྟོགས་ཤིག་ཅེས་བསྟན་པའི་འདོམས་པ་ཡིན་པའི་ཕྱིར།](quote:p402-q18). He adds that the attachment arises from the merit of body and the rest attached to ordinary activity (བྱ་བ་ཕལ་པ). The paragraph takes over his "not clinging to bad activity", and p403 renders his "ultimately" qualifier (དོན་དམ་པར) as བདེན་མེད. **Khedrup Je**, in the ཕར་ཕྱིན་རྟོགས་དཀའི་སྣང་བ, supplies the paragraph's exact label: [བྱ་བ་ངན་ཞེན་གྱི་ལེ་ལོའི་གཉེན་པོ](quote:p402-q17), explained as clinging to the ordinary pleasures of body and the rest. **Panchen Sonam Drakpa**'s འགྲེལ་ཚིག་དོན་རབ་གསལ། lists "the exertion of non-attachment" among the ten instructions (at character 44150) without naming the laziness. On this point it offers nothing to compare.

**Sera Jey specificity.** Nothing in the doctrine here is distinctive to Sera Jey. Tsongkhapa, Khedrup Je and later Ngawang Palden all read the three instructions as antidotes to three lazinesses; Gyaltsab's gloss at this point does not use the frame, but does not contradict it. Two things are characteristic of this yig cha. The first is the rigid two-part template, *sūtra first, treatise second*, applied even to an instruction whose treatise gloss is a single clause of Haribhadra's. It trains the student to know the exact sūtra span behind every lemma, delimited by Haribhadra's own markers. The second is the devotional excursus on refuge, which Chökyi Gyaltsen shares with Chöjor Pelsang but which Tsongkhapa's gser phreng and Gyaltsab's རྣམ་བཤད do not have at this place.

## 5 · Bones of Contention 🦴

**What is disputed?** The paragraph argues with nobody; there is no ཁ་ཅིག. But its wording takes a side on three points on which the sources it depends on do not agree, and its attribution of the verse differs from a parallel commentary's.

**1 · What the first laziness is called.** The paragraph says the instruction abandons **བྱ་བ་ངན་ཞེན་གྱི་ལེ་ལོ**, the laziness of attachment to bad activity. Tsongkhapa's gser phreng says it abandons **སྙོམས་ལས་འཛིན་པ**, clinging to idleness ([q13](quote:p402-q13)). So does Chökyi Gyaltsen himself in his outline of this very line earlier in the text ([q16](quote:p402-q16)). Khedrup Je ([q17](quote:p402-q17)) and, later, Ngawang Palden use the paragraph's label, and Gyaltsab's gloss ([q18](quote:p402-q18)) points the same way. Śāntideva's first half-line ([q15](quote:p402-q15)), ལེ་ལོ་ངན་ལ་ཞེན་པ, can be parsed as laziness together with attachment to the bad, and his very next verse turns to the taste of idleness (སྙོམ་ལས་བདེ་བའི་རོ). Since the Ornament has only three exertions, any reading must fold two of Śāntideva's items into one or split them differently. Tsongkhapa's label and the Khedrup/Gyaltsab label each pick out one of the two. *Why it matters:* the two labels describe different faults. Idleness is a lack of effort; "attachment to bad activity", as Khedrup and Gyaltsab gloss it, is effort misdirected towards the pleasure of ordinary activity. Only the second matches what the sūtra and Haribhadra describe: a bodhisattva who *has* taken up exertion and then clings to the well-being it brings. The paragraph's label fits the text it introduces better than the text's own outline does. That is a real, if small, inconsistency inside Chökyi Gyaltsen's own work, and I report it rather than resolve it. I did not locate the lam rim's standard account of the three lazinesses in the Tsongkhapa volumes searched, so I cannot say how either label relates to it.

**2 · Blameworthy action: vice, or reification?** Read naively, "no opening to blameworthy actions" is a moral rule. Daṃṣṭrasena says explicitly that it is not: ཁ་ན་མ་ཐོ་བ here is objectifying action, not sin ([q12](quote:p402-q12)). The sūtra's own answer ([q09](quote:p402-q09)) supports him. Haribhadra ([q07](quote:p402-q07)), Tsongkhapa and Gyaltsab all take the remedy to be seeing that body and the rest lack own-nature. The paragraph's label "attachment to bad activity" could be misread as the moral reading. The paragraph does not decide the matter itself; it waits for p403, which resolves it along Daṃṣṭrasena's and Gyaltsab's lines (the sūtra teaches body, speech and so on as equally without true existence). The Indian and Tibetan sources agree here. The only risk is a student taking the label for the content.

**3 · བསོད་ནམས or བསོད་ཉམས.** In Haribhadra's gloss as it stands in this archive the object of the clinging is the **བསོད་ནམས** (merit) of body and the rest ([q07](quote:p402-q07)). Tsongkhapa reads **བསོད་ཉམས** and glosses it as the pleasure that arises (བདེ་བ་སྐྱེས་པ) ([q14](quote:p402-q14)). Chökyi Gyaltsen in p403 follows Tsongkhapa: he quotes Haribhadra with བསོད་ཉམས and explains it as the pleasure (བདེ་བ) of body, speech and mind. Gyaltsab and Khedrup keep བསོད་ནམས. Khedrup nonetheless explains it as attachment to ordinary *pleasure*, so in substance he lands where Tsongkhapa does. This is a disagreement about the reading of an Indian text, and it matters for the doctrine. Clinging to *merit* is a subtle, Madhyamaka-flavoured fault; clinging to *comfort* makes it an ordinary obstacle to effort. The Sera Jey reading follows Tsongkhapa. As the rubric requires, I do not speculate on which reading the Sanskrit supports.

**4 · Whose verse?** The paragraph gives the verse to རྗེ་བདག་ཉིད་ཆེན་པོ. Chöjor Pelsang, in an otherwise almost identical passage, has བརྩེ་བ་ཆེན་པོས་གསུངས ([q04](quote:p402-q04)). The colophon of the work in Tsongkhapa's collected works ([q02](quote:p402-q02)) supports the paragraph: the verse is Tsongkhapa's. I cannot tell from the evidence whether བརྩེ་བ་ཆེན་པོ in Chöjor Pelsang is an epithet for the same person or a different attribution.

**Resolution.** This yig cha places the instruction in the Pañcaviṃśati as Haribhadra segmented it. It names the laziness in Khedrup's and Gyaltsab's terms rather than in the gser phreng's (while its own outline keeps the gser phreng's), and in the next paragraph it follows Tsongkhapa's reading བསོད་ཉམས. On the evidence available, the paragraph's choice of label is the better fit to the sūtra it introduces.

## 6 · Key Terminology & Technical Concepts

- **ཐུན་མོང་བའི་བསླབ་བྱ།** — the common trainings of refuge, the ones that apply to all three Jewels together, as against the specific (སོ་སོའི) trainings owed to each Jewel separately.
  - **How understood here:** the fifth heading of the refuge excursus is divided [specific / common](quote:p402-q05). The specific ones (p401) are mostly prohibitions tied to one Jewel each. The common ones here are all positive and inward: recollection, respect, and never admitting disbelief.
  - **Debates:** none raised in these sources. The list is traditional, and its twin in Chöjor Pelsang ([q03](quote:p402-q03)) differs only in wording.

- **ཁ་ན་མ་ཐོ་བ་དང་བཅས་པའི་ལས།** — "blameworthy" action.
  - **Sanskrit equivalent:** *sāvadya karman*.
  - **How understood here:** not unwholesome action but action performed while apprehending its agent and instrument as real. The sūtra defines it as the very thought "what is the body by which I exert myself?". Daṃṣṭrasena says so in as many words ([q12](quote:p402-q12)).
  - **Debates:** the ordinary Abhidharma sense (morally faulty) is exactly what the Indian commentator rules out for this passage. See §5.2.

- **མ་ཞེན་པའི་བརྩོན་འགྲུས།** — the exertion of non-attachment, the first of the three exertions among the ten instructions.
  - **How understood here:** exertion that does not cling to the well-being its own practice produces, because it sees the practising body, speech and mind as without own-nature.
  - **Debates:** which laziness it opposes: སྙོམས་ལས་འཛིན་པ (Tsongkhapa; Chökyi Gyaltsen's outline) or བྱ་བ་ངན་ཞེན (this paragraph; Khedrup). See §5.1.

- **བྱ་བ་ངན་ཞེན་གྱི་ལེ་ལོ།** — the laziness of attachment to bad or ordinary activity: a laziness that is busy rather than idle, and busy with the wrong thing.
  - **How understood here:** Khedrup glosses it as attachment to the ordinary pleasures of body and the rest ([q17](quote:p402-q17)), and Gyaltsab as attachment arising from activity of the ordinary kind (བྱ་བ་ཕལ་པ). That is why it can be remedied by an instruction about the emptiness of body, speech and mind.

- **བསོད་ཉམས / བསོད་ནམས།** — the well-being (or merit) of body and the rest to which one becomes attached.
  - **How understood here:** read བསོད་ཉམས and glossed as pleasure (བདེ་བ) by Tsongkhapa ([q14](quote:p402-q14)) and, in p403, by Chökyi Gyaltsen.
  - **Debates:** the reading of Haribhadra's text itself. See §5.3.

- **ལེའུ་བརྒྱད་མ།** — Haribhadra's eight-chapter rearrangement of the Pañcaviṃśatisāhasrikā, aligned with the eight topics of the Ornament and carrying his own section-markers (དགེ་འདུན་དཀོན་མཆོག་ལ་གདམས་པའོ། …མ་ཞེན་པ་ལ་གདམས་པའོ།).
  - **How understood here:** it is the "sūtra" this yig cha quotes. Its wording, not the Kangyur Pañcaviṃśati's ([q10](quote:p402-q10)), is what appears in the paragraph, and its markers define the span of sūtra that a lemma of the Ornament covers.
