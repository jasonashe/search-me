# The ultimate refuge is the Buddha alone: the Uttaratantra verse and what "the two truths" means in it

## 1 · Passage & Context

**Source Text:**

> གཉིས་པ་ནི། འོ་ན། དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས་དེ་གང་ཞེ་ན། དེ་ཡོད་དེ། སངས་རྒྱས་ཉག་གཅིག་མཐར་ཐུག་པའི་སྐྱབས་ཡིན་པའི་ཕྱིར་ཏེ། དེ་ནི་བདེན་གཉིས་ཀྱི་ཡོན་ཏན་མཐར་ཕྱིན་པའི་ཆོས་ཀྱི་རྟེན་ཅན་ཡིན་པའི་ཕྱིར་ཏེ། རྒྱུད་བླ་མ་ལས། དམ་པའི་དོན་དུ་འགྲོ་བ་ཡི། ། སྐྱབས་ནི་སངས་རྒྱས་ཉག་གཅིག་ཡིན། ། ཐུབ་པ་ཆོས་ཀྱི་སྐུ་ཅན་ཕྱིར༑ ༑ ཞེས་གསུངས་པའི་ཕྱིར།

**Preceding Section:** p396 opens the heading གསུམ་པ་ཀུན་རྫོབ་པའི་སྐྱབས་དང་དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས་ཀྱི་ཁྱད་པར་བཤད་པ and handles the first of its two parts, conventional refuge. It quotes the Uttaratantra verse སྤང་ཕྱིར་བསླུ་བའི་ཆོས་ཅན་ཕྱིར། ། མེད་ཕྱིར་འཇིགས་དང་བཅས་པའི་ཕྱིར། ། ཆོས་རྣམ་གཉིས་དང་འཕགས་པའི་ཚོགས། ། གཏན་གྱི་སྐྱབས་མཆོག་མ་ཡིན་ནོ། and then works through each of its four reasons, each supported by Asaṅga's commentary (རྒྱུད་བླ་མའི་འགྲེལ་པ). The twelve branches of scripture in a disciple's continuum are not final refuge, because once the meaning is realized they are abandoned like a raft; the Mahāsūtrālaṃkāra's གཟིངས་དང་འདྲ supports this. The learner's truth of path is not final refuge, because it appears free of all stains without being so, and so is deceptive; it is also impermanent, since it has to change into a stainless state. Nirvāṇa without remainder is not final refuge, because it is marked off only by the mere absence of afflictions and suffering. Tenth-ground bodhisattvas and Hīnayāna arhats are not final refuge, because they still carry the fear that comes from the obscurations to omniscience, like someone who stands before an executioner with a raised sword.

**Following Section:** p398 answers the obvious objection that if the final refuge is the Buddha alone, the final refuge lacks the three jewels. It completes the verse this paragraph breaks off, quoting its fourth line ཚོགས་ཀྱང་དེ་ཡི་མཐར་ཐུག་ཕྱིར། directly and again through the གསེར་ཕྲེང: the Saṅgha of the three vehicles reaches its culmination in the attainment of buddhahood. It then gives the section's definitions. Conventional refuge is a refuge that, as things actually stand, has not completed the journey of the path, and is synonymous with temporary refuge. Ultimate refuge is a refuge that has completed that journey, and is synonymous with final refuge. It adds that no conventional refuge has an ultimate refuge as itself or as one of its parts. p399 then turns to the author of the རོང་ཊཱིཀ, who denied final-refuge status to the scriptural Dharma, the learner's path, the learner's cessation and the learner Saṅgha. The yig cha turns his reasons back on him: the Buddha's own speech, the two form bodies and even the svābhāvikakāya would all fail by them. It ends by quoting Gyaltsab Je's condemnation of the view that everything composite is excluded from final refuge.

**What this paragraph is doing.** This is the positive half of a two-part heading. p396 said what the ultimate refuge is not, and this paragraph says what it is. It makes three moves in 306 characters. It establishes that an ultimate refuge exists, answering the implicit worry that p396's eliminations leave nothing. It identifies that refuge with the Buddha alone, and in doing so treats "ultimate" and "final" as the same thing: the reason it gives for the Buddha being the *ultimate* refuge is that the Buddha is the *final* refuge. It grounds this in one reason, that the Buddha possesses the culminated qualities of "the two truths," and in one scriptural authority, the Uttaratantra. It deliberately stops the verse a line short and leaves ཚོགས་ཀྱང་དེ་ཡི་མཐར་ཐུག་ཕྱིར for p398, where it is needed to answer an objection.

**On "the two truths" (བདེན་གཉིས).** This is the one place where the paragraph can easily be misread. The whole heading is about the difference between conventional (ཀུན་རྫོབ) and ultimate (དོན་དམ) refuge, so a reader naturally takes བདེན་གཉིས to be conventional and ultimate truth. That is not where the phrase comes from. The reason clause is Chökyi Gyaltsen's compression of Asaṅga's commentary on this same verse, which glosses ཐུབ་པ་ཆོས་ཀྱི་སྐུ་ཅན་ཕྱིར as [the Sage possessing the dharmakāya that is freedom from attachment, characterized by the two truths of purification](quote:p397-q02). Earlier in the same commentary Asaṅga defines those two truths: [འདོད་ཆགས་དང་བྲལ་བ་གང་ཡིན་པ་འགོག་པའི་བདེན་པ་དང། གང་གིས་འདོད་ཆགས་དང་བྲལ་བ་ལམ་གྱི་བདེན་པ་དེ་གཉིས་ཀ་མངོན་པར་བསྡུས་ཏེ། རྣམ་པར་བྱང་བའི་བདེན་པ་གཉིས་ཀྱི་མཚན་ཉིད་ཀྱི་འདོད་ཆགས་དང་བྲལ་བའི་ཆོས་ཡིན་ནོ་ཞེས་བསྟན་ཏོ། །](quote:p397-q03). The freedom from attachment is the truth of cessation, and that by which one is freed is the truth of path, and together they are "the two truths of purification." So བདེན་གཉིས་ཀྱི་ཡོན་ཏན་མཐར་ཕྱིན་པའི་ཆོས means the Dharma Jewel as cessation and path brought to completion. Tsongkhapa reads the verse in exactly this way (§4). The yig cha does not spell this out, and I am supplying the reading from its source. It is not a gloss Chökyi Gyaltsen gives here.

**On ཆོས་ཀྱི་རྟེན་ཅན.** The verse has ཆོས་ཀྱི་སྐུ་ཅན, "possessing the dharmakāya." The yig cha's prose has ཆོས་ཀྱི་རྟེན་ཅན, which reads most naturally as "that which is the basis of the Dharma." That fits the reading above: the Buddha is the person in whose continuum the completed cessation and path, the ultimate Dharma Jewel, are found. That is also why p398 can say the three jewels are not lost when "the Buddha alone" is named. I cannot rule out that རྟེན is a scribal variant for སྐུ, and no other witness to this sentence was found, so the reading "basis of the Dharma" should be held lightly.

**On the verse's wording.** The yig cha's text matches the Tengyur root text exactly: [དམ་པའི་དོན་དུ་འགྲོ་བ་ཡི། །སྐྱབས་ནི་སངས་རྒྱས་ཉག་གཅིག་ཡིན། །ཐུབ་པ་ཆོས་ཀྱི་སྐུ་ཅན་ཕྱིར། །ཚོགས་ཀྱང་དེ་ཡི་མཐར་ཐུག་ཕྱིར། །](quote:p397-q01). When Asaṅga's commentary embeds the verse, the Derge text reads ཉག་གཅིག་ཉིད and ཚོགས་ཀྱང་དེ་ཡིན, but this has no bearing on the argument. For the first line, Jamyang Zhepa gives the gloss the translation above follows: [དམ་པའི་དོན་ནི་དགོས་དོན་མཆོག་དང་འདོད་དོན་མཆོག་ཡིན། འགྲོ་བ་ནི་དེར་ཕྱིན་ཟིན་པའི་སྐྱབས་ཞེས་པའི་དོན་ཡིན་པའི་ཕྱིར།](quote:p397-q17). On his reading, དམ་པའི་དོན is the supreme purpose, and འགྲོ་བ is not "beings" but "the refuge that has already arrived there." That reading would make the line "the refuge that has gone to the supreme goal is the Buddha alone," which fits the paragraph's equation of ultimate with *final* refuge even more closely. I have kept "beings" in the translation because it is the ordinary construal, but Jamyang Zhepa's reading is the one that explains why the yig cha treats the verse as proving finality.

## 3 · Structural Context (Dkar Chag)

- **Abhisamayālaṃkāra location:** Chapter 1, the ten instructions (གདམས་ངག), in the verse that lists them. The whole refuge excursus hangs on the third item: [སངས་རྒྱས་ལ་སོགས་དཀོན་མཆོག་གསུམ། ། མ་ཞེན་ཡོངས་སུ་མི་ངལ་དང་། །](quote:p397-q19). The Ornament names the three jewels as a subject of instruction and says nothing more about them.
- **Haribhadra's commentary location:** at the same lemma in the འགྲེལ་པ་དོན་གསལ, I read Haribhadra's gloss in full. His entire treatment of the Buddha Jewel is this: [སངས་རྒྱས་ལ་སོགས་སངས་རྒྱས་དང་བྱང་ཆུབ་དག་མཚན་ཉིད་ཀྱིས་སངས་རྒྱས་སུ་བྱེད་པའི་ཆོས་ཀྱི་མཚན་ཉིད་རྣམ་པ་ཐམས་ཅད་མཁྱེན་པ་ཉིད་མི་དམིགས་པ་ལ་གཟུགས་ལ་སོགས་པ་མི་སྦྱོར་བས་དམིགས་པར་བྱ་བ་དང་དམིགས་པར་བྱེད་པ་ཉིད་དུ་ཤེས་པ་ཞེས་བྱ་བ་དང་།](quote:p397-q18). He moves straight on to the Dharma, the Saṅgha of twenty, and the next instruction. The instruction about the Buddha is an instruction to practise non-apprehension. He draws no distinction between conventional and ultimate refuge, does not ask which of the three jewels is final, and does not cite the Uttaratantra. The whole question of p396–p399 comes into this curriculum from the Uttaratantra and Asaṅga's commentary on it, by way of the Tibetan commentators, and not from Haribhadra. That Haribhadra is silent here is a real finding: this section is Tibetan scholastic material grafted onto one word of the Ornament.
- **Text location:** སྐབས་དང་པོའི་སྤྱི་དོན, under the section སྐྱབས་གསུམ་ལ་གདམས་པ།སངས་རྒྱས་ལ་སོགས་དཀོན་མཆོག་གསུམ། ། ཞེས་སོགས་ཀྱི་སྐབས་སུ་གཉིས།, which opens at p376 (page 273). Within it, this paragraph falls under the sub-topic གསུམ་པ་ཀུན་རྫོབ་པའི་སྐྱབས་དང་དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས་ཀྱི་ཁྱད་པར་བཤད་པ, which opens at p396 (page 284). This paragraph opens the second of that sub-topic's two parts, ༢༽ དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས. p398 continues that part without a new heading. I did not reconstruct what the first two members of the division ending in གསུམ་པ are, and the pack does not give them.
- **Section type:** own-system exposition (རང་ལུགས), in answer to an implicit question (འོ་ན … གང་ཞེ་ན), supported by a single scriptural proof.
- **Argumentative role:** it supplies the positive term that p396's four eliminations were clearing space for. It also commits the text to treating ultimate and final refuge as synonyms, and that commitment decides the controversies in p398 and p399. If "ultimate refuge" were a separate category from "final refuge," the learner's ultimate Dharma and Saṅgha could count as ultimate refuge without being final. The yig cha closes that door here, by giving finality as the reason for ultimacy.

## 4 · Philosophical Exposition & Commentary

**Core meaning.** Asaṅga sets up the whole structure the paragraph relies on. He says that the other two refuges are not refuge at the time of culmination (མཐར་ཐུག་པའི་དུས་ན), and then: [དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས་ནི་གཅིག་ཉིད་དེ། འདི་ལྟ་སྟེ། དེ་བཞིན་གཤེགས་པ་དགྲ་བཅོམ་པ་ཡང་དག་པར་རྫོགས་པའི་སངས་རྒྱས་སོ། །](quote:p397-q04): "the ultimate refuge is only one, namely the Tathāgata, the arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha." He then refers the reader to the Śrīmālā sūtra. (I did not locate the corresponding Śrīmālā passage in the Kangyur, and nothing is said about it here beyond Asaṅga's reference.) Chökyi Gyaltsen's paragraph restates this. Only the Buddha is ultimate refuge, because only the Buddha is final: nothing remains to abandon and nothing remains to realize. What makes the Buddha final is that the two truths of purification, cessation and path, are complete in the Buddha.

**Je Tsongkhapa's reading, and why it matters for "the two truths."** In the ལེགས་བཤད་གསེར་ཕྲེང, under the heading སངས་རྒྱས་འབའ་ཞིག་གཏན་གྱི་སྐྱབས་ཡིན་ནོ།, Tsongkhapa answers the objection p398 will answer. He does so by reading the verse's dharmakāya as [སངས་རྒྱས་ཀྱི་ཐུགས་རྒྱུད་ཀྱི་འགོག་ལམ་ཆོས་ཡིན་ལ། ཆོས་ཀྱི་སྐུ་ཐོབ་པ་ན་ཐེག་པ་གསུམ་གྱི་ཚོགས་ཀྱང་མཐར་ཕྱིན་པར་འགྱུར་བའི་ཕྱིར་ཏེ།](quote:p397-q05): "the cessation and path in the Buddha's continuum are the Dharma; and when the dharmakāya is attained, the Saṅgha of the three vehicles too reaches completion." This is the key to the paragraph's reason clause. "The two truths" are cessation and path, and the Buddha is their basis. Elsewhere in the same work, arguing against those who deny that a Buddha has a path at all, Tsongkhapa uses the identical gloss of Asaṅga's as his proof text: [རྣམ་པར་བྱང་བའི་བདེན་པ་གཉིས་ཀྱི་མཚན་ཉིད་འདོད་ཆགས་དང་བྲལ་བའི་ཆོས་ཀྱི་སྐུ་ཅན་ཞེས་གསུངས་པའི་ཕྱིར་དང་། གཞན་ཡང་སངས་རྒྱས་ཀྱི་འདུས་བྱས་ཀྱི་རྟོགས་པ་དེ་དག་ལམ་མིན་ན་བདེན་པ་བཞི་གང་དུ་བཞག བདེན་པ་བཞི་གང་ཡང་མིན་པའི་འཕགས་པའི་མངོན་རྟོགས་ཡོད་པ་འདི་ནི་བློ་གྲོས་ནམ་མཁའ་དང་མཉམ་པ](quote:p397-q06). "If those composite realizations of the Buddha are not path, among which of the four truths will you put them? A noble realization that is none of the four truths is something only a mind as vast as the sky could find." So in the Gelug reading, the dharmakāya that makes the Buddha the ultimate refuge *includes conditioned wisdom*, the Buddha's own truth of path. This is not a side point. It is what p399 defends against the author of the རོང་ཊཱིཀ.

**Gyaltsab Je.** The རྣམ་བཤད་སྙིང་པོའི་རྒྱན draws the conclusion explicitly: [སྐུ་གསུམ་གྱི་བདག་ཉིད་ཅན་གྱི་སངས་རྒྱས་ཁོ་ན་དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས་སོ། །](quote:p397-q07). The ultimate refuge is "the Buddha who is the nature of the three kāyas," so the two form bodies are included. He is harsh with the opposite view: [འདུས་བྱས་ཡིན་ན་དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས་མ་ཡིན་པས་ཁྱབ་པ་བསྟན་པར་འདོད་པ་ནི། སངས་རྒྱས་ཀྱི་ལམ་བདེན་དང་། གཟུགས་སྐུ་རྣམ་པ་གཉིས་མཐར་ཐུག་གི་སྐྱབས་ཡིན་པ་འགོག་པའི་ལོག་རྟོག་གི་ཐ་ཆད་དུ་ཤེས་པར་བྱ་ལ་རྒྱང་རིང་དུ་སྤང་བརབྱའོ། །](quote:p397-q08). Anyone who takes the verse to teach that whatever is composite is not ultimate refuge holds "the lowest of wrong views" and is to be avoided from far off. The yig cha quotes this sentence verbatim at the end of p399. Gyaltsab Je is also the source of the definitions p398 gives: [དངོས་པོའི་གནས་ཚོད་ལ་ལམ་གྱི་བགྲོད་པ་མཐར་མ་ཐུག་པའི་སྐྱབས་དེ་ཀུན་རྫོབ་པའི་སྐྱབས་དང་། བསམ་ངོར་ཡང་བགྲོད་པ་མཐར་ཐུག་པར་འཛིན་ཅིང་། དོན་ལ་ཡང་མཐར་ཐུག་པ་དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས་ཀྱི་དོན་ཡིན་གྱི།](quote:p397-q09). In that account, conventional versus ultimate is a matter of whether the path has *actually* been completed (དངོས་པོའི་གནས་ཚོད་ལ). It is not about which of the two truths a refuge belongs to. That is exactly why ultimate and final can be equated.

**Khedrup Je** makes the same point by reductio in the ཕར་ཕྱིན་རྟོགས་དཀའི་སྣང་བ: [གང་དག་དེ་ནི་འདུས་བྱས་ཡིན་ལ། འདུས་བྱས་ཡིན་ན་རྫུན་པ་བསླུ་བའི་ཆོས་ཅན་ཡིན་པས་མཐར་ཐུག་གི་སྐྱབས་སུ་མི་རིགས་པའི་ཕྱིར། ཞེས་སྨྲ་བ་ལ་ནི། གཟུགས་སྐུ་གཉིས་ཀྱང་མཐར་ཐུག་གི་སྐྱབས་མིན་པར་ཐལ་ལོ། །](quote:p397-q16). If the learner's path failed as final refuge simply because it is composite and therefore deceptive, the two form bodies would fail too. He concludes in the same words as this paragraph: དེས་ན་སངས་རྒྱས་ཉག་གཅིག་མཐར་ཐུག་གི་སྐྱབས་སུ་འཐད་དོ།

**Jetsun Chökyi Gyaltsen's own view elsewhere.** His སྐབས་དང་པོའི་མཐའ་དཔྱོད uses this paragraph's verse as a pervasion in live debate. The opponent there argues [ཁ་ཅིག གཞུང་འདི་རྣམས་ཀྱིས་གནས་སྐབས་ཀྱི་སྐྱབས་དང་མཐར་ཐུག་གི་སྐྱབས་ཀྱི་ཁྱད་པར་སྟོན་པ་ཡིན་གྱི། ཀུན་རྫོབ་པའི་སྐྱབས་དང་། དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས་ཀྱི་ཁྱད་པར་མི་སྟོན་ལ། དེས་ན་གཟུགས་སྐུ་གཉིས་ཀུན་རྫོབ་པའི་སྐྱབས་དང་། ཐེག་ཆེན་གྱི་མཐོང་ལམ་བར་ཆད་མེད་ལམ་དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས་ཡིན། ཞེས་ཟེར།](quote:p397-q10). On this view, the Uttaratantra verses mark temporary against final refuge, and "conventional" and "ultimate" refuge are a separate division, by which the form bodies are conventional and the Mahāyāna uninterrupted path of seeing is ultimate. Chökyi Gyaltsen answers with the verse itself: [ཐེག་ཆེན་གྱི་མཐོང་ལམ་ཆོས་ཅན། སངས་རྒྱས་དཀོན་མཆོག་ཡིན་པར་ཐལ། དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས་ཡིན་པའི་ཕྱིར། རྟགས་ཁས། ཁྱབ་སྟེ། རྒྱུད་བླ་མ་ལས། དམ་པའི་དོན་དུ་འགྲོ་བ་ཡི། ། སྐྱབས་ནི་སངས་རྒྱས་ཉག་གཅིག་ཡིན། ། ཞེས་བཤད་པའི་ཕྱིར།](quote:p397-q11). If the path of seeing were ultimate refuge, it would have to be the Buddha Jewel, because the verse says the ultimate refuge is the Buddha alone. He then states his own system in the terms this paragraph assumes: [རང་ལུགས་ལ། དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས་དང་མཐར་ཐུག་གི་སྐྱབས་དོན་གཅིག་ཀུན་རྫོབ་པའི་སྐྱབས་དང་གནས་སྐབས་ཀྱི་སྐྱབས་དོན་གཅིག་བྱས་པ་ལ་ཁོ་ན་རེ། ལོངས་སྐུ་ཆོས་ཅན། དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐུ་ཡིན་པར་ཐལ། དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས་ཡིན་པའི་ཕྱིར།](quote:p397-q12). Ultimate refuge and final refuge are synonyms, and so are conventional and temporary refuge. The objection this draws is telling: then the saṃbhogakāya, being ultimate refuge, would be an "ultimate body" (དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐུ). He answers that there is no pervasion. "Ultimate" in "ultimate refuge" does not carry over into "ultimate body." The parallel with this paragraph's བདེན་གཉིས, which is likewise not the two truths of Madhyamaka, is mine. The mtha' dpyod passage shows how much weight the paragraph's short proof is expected to bear.

**Panchen Sonam Drakpa.** I searched his འགྲེལ་ཚིག་དོན་རབ་གསལ at the aligned lemma (སངས་རྒྱས་ལ་སོགས་དཀོན་མཆོག་གསུམ) and for the verse and the terms དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས and མཐར་ཐུག་གི་སྐྱབས་ཡིན, and found none of them. As a word-gloss on the root text and Haribhadra, it follows Haribhadra in passing over the question. His spyi don is not among the sources here, so the Loseling position on this paragraph was not located.

**Sera Jey specificity.** On the substance there is no difference between Sera Jey and Drepung Gomang. Jamyang Zhepa also equates the terms (མཐར་ཐུག་པའི་སྐྱབས་དང་། དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས་དང་། བདེན་པའི་སྐྱབས་དང་། མི་བསླུ་བའི་སྐྱབས་རྣམས་དོན་གཅིག་སྟེ) and says ultimate refuge exists only at the stage of no-more-learning. Both houses follow Gyaltsab Je. What is characteristic of Chökyi Gyaltsen is the economy. Gyaltsab and Tsongkhapa argue the point at length. The yig cha gives the thesis, a single reason compressed from Asaṅga, and the verse, and it saves the argument for p399 and for the mtha' dpyod. Jamyang Zhepa does record a ཁ་ཅིག, unnamed, who objects that conventional and ultimate refuge cannot be explained *only* as temporary and final, because the pair also carries the senses "nominal refuge" (ཐ་སྙད་པའི་སྐྱབས) and "true refuge" (བདེན་པའི་སྐྱབས). The Sera Jey yig cha does not raise this objection here.

## 5 · Bones of Contention 🦴

**What is disputed?** The paragraph reads as a simple identification, but it takes a side on two questions. Is ultimate refuge the same as final refuge? And is the "Buddha alone" of the verse the Buddha's dharmakāya only, excluding everything composite, or the Buddha as a whole, including the Buddha's path truth and the form bodies?

**Who argues what?**

- **Jetsun Chökyi Gyaltsen, with Tsongkhapa, Gyaltsab Je and Khedrup Je:** ultimate = final. The Buddha alone is both, and "the Buddha" includes the completed path (Tsongkhapa's སངས་རྒྱས་ཀྱི་འདུས་བྱས་ཀྱི་རྟོགས་པ) and the three kāyas (Gyaltsab's སྐུ་གསུམ་གྱི་བདག་ཉིད་ཅན). Being composite is no bar.
- **The ལུང་རིགས་ཀྱི་རོལ་མཚོ, catalogued under Ngok Lotsāwa Loden Sherab:** this is a pre-Gelug reading, at a real distance from this curriculum. At this very place the text reports "the great Lotsāwa" (བདག་ཉིད་ཆེན་པོ་ལོ་ཙཱ་བ) in the third person, so its voice may be a later member of his exegetical line, and I attribute it to the text rather than to Ngok himself. It takes the verse to name the dharmakāya alone: [གཉིས་པ་ནི་གོང་དུ་བཤད་པའི་མཚན་ཉིད་ཅན་གྱི་སངས་རྒྱས་ཀྱི་ཆོས་ཀྱི་སྐུ་ཉག་གཅིག་དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས་དང་། མཐར་ཐུག་གི་སྐྱབས་གཉིས་ཀ་ཡིན་ཏེ།](quote:p397-q13). It explicitly excludes the form bodies: [གཟུགས་ཀྱི་སྐུ་གཉིས་པོ་ཆོས་ཅན། དོན་དམ་པ་དང་མཐར་ཐུག་གི་སྐྱབས་མ་ཡིན་ཏེ། ཀུན་རྫོབ་ཀྱི་བདེན་པ་ཡིན་པའི་ཕྱིར་དང་།](quote:p397-q14). Here the two truths of Madhyamaka *are* doing the work: form bodies are conventional truths and therefore not ultimate refuge. The same text also *separates* ultimate from final. It says the ultimate Dharma and Saṅgha of learners [are ultimate refuge](quote:p397-q15) because they are ultimate truth, and adds immediately that they are not final refuge, because realization and abandonment still have further to go. It also calls the reading that makes the form bodies final refuge རྣམ་པར་འཁྱམས, "wandering astray." This is the precise opposite of Gyaltsab Je's ལོག་རྟོག་གི་ཐ་ཆད. Each side calls the other's reading of the same four lines the worst possible error.
- **The ཁ་ཅིག of Chökyi Gyaltsen's mtha' dpyod:** holds a third position. The verses divide temporary from final refuge, conventional and ultimate refuge are a separate division, the form bodies are conventional refuge, and the path of seeing is ultimate refuge. This is close to the ལུང་རིགས་ཀྱི་རོལ་མཚོ in using the two truths to sort refuges, but it does not share its view of finality.
- **The author of the རོང་ཊཱིཀ (p399):** denies final-refuge status to every learner-level jewel, on the ground of deceptiveness, non-affirming negation and so on. The yig cha does not quarrel with those conclusions. It quarrels with the reasons, which would equally disqualify the Buddha's own speech and form bodies.

**Why does it matter?** Because it decides what "the Buddha" in "the Buddha alone" refers to. If ultimate refuge is defined by ultimate *truth*, the rūpakāyas, which are conventional truths and exist for others' benefit, fall outside it. The refuge a practitioner actually faces is then only the unconditioned dharmakāya, and the Buddha who teaches is demoted to a conventional refuge. If ultimate refuge is defined by *completion of the path*, as in Gyaltsab Je's definition and p398, then everything about the Buddha, conditioned or not, is ultimate refuge, because none of it needs to go further. The paragraph's reason, "the Buddha is the basis of the Dharma in which *cessation and path* are perfected," only works on the second reading. A truth of path is composite, and if composites were excluded, the reason would undercut its own conclusion. This is why the reading of བདེན་གཉིས in §2 is not a pedantic point. If one takes it as conventional and ultimate truth, the paragraph drifts toward the Ngok-lineage position it is meant to exclude.

**Resolution.** Sera Jey follows Gyaltsab Je. Ultimate and final refuge are synonyms, defined by whether the path has actually been completed. The Buddha alone meets that definition, and "the Buddha" includes the conditioned path of no-more-learning and the form bodies. The Uttaratantra is read through Asaṅga's gloss, where the dharmakāya is characterized by the two truths of *purification*. That reading has the Indian commentary on its side, which the rival reading does not: in the passages of his commentary read here, Asaṅga never says the form bodies are excluded because they are conventional truths. The Ngok-lineage text's reading has to import the Madhyamaka two truths into a verse whose own commentator glosses "two truths" as cessation and path. The weight of evidence here is good. The root verse and its commentary are canonical, and the Gelug position is stated by three tier-2 masters as well as by Chökyi Gyaltsen himself in his mtha' dpyod. Only the dissenting reading rests on a single tier-5 text.

## 6 · Key Terminology & Technical Concepts

- **དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས། / མཐར་ཐུག་གི་སྐྱབས།** — ultimate refuge and final refuge.
  - **Sanskrit equivalent:** *paramārtha-śaraṇa*; the verse's དམ་པའི་དོན་དུ renders *paramārthena*.
  - **How understood here:** synonyms. The paragraph proves the first from the second. "Ultimate" here means "at the culmination" and "in the supreme sense." It does not mean "belonging to ultimate truth."
  - **Debates:** the ལུང་རིགས་ཀྱི་རོལ་མཚོ separates them, so that learners' ultimate Dharma and Saṅgha are ultimate but not final. Chökyi Gyaltsen's mtha' dpyod has to defend the identification against the objection that the saṃbhogakāya would then be an "ultimate body."

- **རྣམ་པར་བྱང་བའི་བདེན་པ་གཉིས།** — the two truths of purification: the truth of cessation (འགོག་བདེན) and the truth of path (ལམ་བདེན).
  - **How understood here:** the referent of the paragraph's བདེན་གཉིས, taken over from Asaṅga's gloss on ཐུབ་པ་ཆོས་ཀྱི་སྐུ་ཅན. It is not the pair conventional truth / ultimate truth.
  - **Debates:** Tsongkhapa uses Asaṅga's phrase to prove there is a true path at buddhahood, against those who confine true path to the paths of seeing and meditation. What is at stake is whether a composite can be ultimate refuge.

- **ཆོས་ཀྱི་སྐུ་ཅན། / ཆོས་ཀྱི་རྟེན་ཅན།** — possessing the dharmakāya; being the basis of the Dharma.
  - **Sanskrit equivalent:** *dharmakāyatvāt* (for the verse's phrase).
  - **How understood here:** the Buddha is the ultimate refuge as the one in whom the Dharma Jewel, cessation and path perfected, is found. This is why, in p398, the Dharma Jewel is not lost when "the Buddha alone" is named.
  - **Debates:** whether dharmakāya here means only the unconditioned svābhāvikakāya, as the ལུང་རིགས་ཀྱི་རོལ་མཚོ reads it, or includes the wisdom dharmakāya and, by extension, the Buddha of three kāyas, as Tsongkhapa and Gyaltsab Je read it.

- **ཉག་གཅིག** — "alone," "the one only."
  - **How understood here:** it excludes the Dharma and Saṅgha *as found in learners*, not the Dharma and Saṅgha as such. p398 makes this explicit with the verse's fourth line.
  - **Debates:** the Ngok-lineage text moves the restriction from "the Buddha" to "the Buddha's dharmakāya," which is a significant narrowing of the same word.
