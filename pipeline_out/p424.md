# The path of meditation, and why the instructions are exactly ten — the close of the first chapter's general meaning

## 1 · Passage & Context

**Source Text:**

> གཉིས་པ་དེའི་དོན་འཆད་པ་ལ། སྒོམ་ཞེས་བྱ་ལ། ཞེས་གསུངས། དེ་ཉིད་ཚིག་དོན་གྱི་སྒོ་ནས་འཆད་པ་ལ། འདུས་བྱས་དང་འདུས་མ་བྱས་དག་ངོ་བོ་གཅིག་པ་ཉིད་ཀྱིས་ཕན་ཚུན་ཐ་དད་པར་གདགས་པར་མི་ནུས་པ་བཞིན་དུ་ཇི་སྐད་བཤད་པའི་མཐོང་བའི་ལམ་གྱིས་མངོན་སུམ་དུ་བྱས་པའི་དངོས་པོ་ལས་ཐ་མི་དད་པ་ལ་དམིགས་པའི་ཕྱིར་མཐོང་བ་དང་སྒོམ་པ་དག་ཐ་དད་དུ་གྱུར་པ་མེད་པས་སྒོམ་པའི་ལམ་མཚན་ཉིད་པ་རྣམ་པར་བཞག་པ་ནི་མེད་ཀྱི། འོན་ཀྱང་དེ་ནི་རྟེན་ཅིང་འབྲེལ་བར་འབྱུང་བའི་ཆོས་ཉིད་ཀྱིས་དེས་སྤང་བར་བྱ་བའི་དངོས་པོའི་གཉེན་པོ་ཉིད་དུ་རྣམ་པར་སྒོམ་པར་བྱེད་དོ། ། ཞེས་སྒོམ་པའི་ལམ་ལ་འདོམས་པ་སྟེ། ཞེས་གསུངས། དོན་ནི། རབ་འབྱོར་འདུས་བྱས་ཀྱི་དབྱིངས་ཞེས་སོགས་ཀྱི་མདོ་ཆོས་ཅན། སྒོམ་ལམ་ལ་གདམས་པ་ཡིན་ཏེ། … འདི་ཙམ་ཁོ་ནས་དོན་ཐམས་ཅད་ཕུན་སུམ་ཚོགས་པར་འགྱུར་བས་གདམས་ངག་ནི་རྣམ་པ་བཅུའོ། ། ཞེས་པའི་གཞུང་འདི་རྣམས་གསུངས་སོ།། །།

(The full paragraph, 3,179 characters, is given unit by unit in section 2.)

**Preceding Section:** p423 (page 318) did the same work for the ninth instruction, the path of seeing. It quoted Haribhadra's gloss on མཐོང་ལམ་དང་། — the sixteen moments of forbearance and knowledge, the yogi who "like a magician" has no clinging to anything — and put the sūtra passage beginning རབ་འབྱོར་བྱང་ཆེན་ཤེར་ཕྱིན་ལ་བརྩམས་ཏེ as the subject of a syllogism: it instructs on the Mahāyāna path of seeing, because it teaches that path as the antidote to the *imputed* (ཀུན་བཏགས) objects of abandonment. It then opened the path-of-meditation topic with its first sub-heading, where the sūtra runs from "Subhūti, without the conditioned element the unconditioned element cannot be designated" to Subhūti's words to Śāriputra that a bodhisattva's fault is craving for dharmas. So p423 gives the sūtra, and p424 gives the treatise's explanation of it (the heading བསྟན་བཅོས་ཀྱིས་འགྲེལ་ཚུལ).

**Following Section:** p425 is not by Chökyi Gyaltsen at all. It is the printers' dedication verses (པར་བྱང་སྨོན་ཚིག), after which the edition has only its apparatus of source and collation notes. **This paragraph is the last paragraph of the སྐབས་དང་པོའི་སྤྱི་དོན།.** The *Ornament* moves straight on to the twenty members of the Saṅgha (དབང་པོ་རྟུལ་དང་རྣོན་པོ་དང་།), and Chökyi Gyaltsen's own treatment of that verse is a separate work, his དགེ་འདུན་ཉི་ཤུའི་མཐའ་གཅོད།, which opens at exactly that line: [དབང་པོ་རྟུལ་དང་རྣོན་པོ་དག ཅེས་སོགས་ཀྱི་སྐབས་སུ། མཚོན་བྱེད་དཔེའི་དགེ་འདུན་བཤད་པ་དང་། མཚོན་བྱ་དོན་གྱི་དགེ་འདུན་བཤད་པ་གཉིས།](quote:p424-q17).

**What this paragraph is doing.** It does two things. First, it explains the tenth instruction, on the path of meditation. It quotes Haribhadra's gloss whole, turns it into a syllogism, and then under two headings gives the nature of the path to be meditated and answers an objection. The objection is this: if the Mahāyāna paths of seeing and meditation are not different in type, they cannot differ in what they are able to abandon. Second, it reads Haribhadra's closing sentence on the ten instructions as the answer to a question about the count (གྲངས་ངེས): why exactly ten? The answer is a fourfold scheme (the nature of practice, its object, its basis, its causes), with the causes divided into three that remove adverse conditions and three that supply favourable ones. Unusually, the exposition comes first and Haribhadra's text is quoted afterwards, as what "these words" (གཞུང་འདི་རྣམས) were spoken to explain.

## 2 · Translation & Breakdown

<!--line 0-51-->
> **གཉིས་པ་དེའི་དོན་འཆད་པ་ལ། སྒོམ་ཞེས་བྱ་ལ། ཞེས་གསུངས།**
>
> Second, [how the treatise] explains its meaning. [The root text] says: "[and] what is called meditation (སྒོམ་ཞེས་བྱ་ལ།)."

<!--line 51-86-->
> **དེ་ཉིད་ཚིག་དོན་གྱི་སྒོ་ནས་འཆད་པ་ལ།**
>
> In explaining that through the meaning of its words, [Haribhadra says]:

<!--line 86-353-->
> **འདུས་བྱས་དང་འདུས་མ་བྱས་དག་ངོ་བོ་གཅིག་པ་ཉིད་ཀྱིས་ཕན་ཚུན་ཐ་དད་པར་གདགས་པར་མི་ནུས་པ་བཞིན་དུ་ཇི་སྐད་བཤད་པའི་མཐོང་བའི་ལམ་གྱིས་མངོན་སུམ་དུ་བྱས་པའི་དངོས་པོ་ལས་ཐ་མི་དད་པ་ལ་དམིགས་པའི་ཕྱིར་མཐོང་བ་དང་སྒོམ་པ་དག་ཐ་དད་དུ་གྱུར་པ་མེད་པས་སྒོམ་པའི་ལམ་མཚན་ཉིད་པ་རྣམ་པར་བཞག་པ་ནི་མེད་ཀྱི།**
>
> "Just as the conditioned and the unconditioned (འདུས་བྱས་དང་འདུས་མ་བྱས), being of one nature, cannot be designated as different from one another, so, because [the path of meditation] focuses on what is not different from the thing made manifest (མངོན་སུམ་དུ་བྱས་པའི་དངོས་པོ) by the path of seeing as already explained, seeing and meditation have not become different. Therefore no defined path of meditation (སྒོམ་པའི་ལམ་མཚན་ཉིད་པ) is set up [as distinct];

<!--line 353-519-->
> **འོན་ཀྱང་དེ་ནི་རྟེན་ཅིང་འབྲེལ་བར་འབྱུང་བའི་ཆོས་ཉིད་ཀྱིས་དེས་སྤང་བར་བྱ་བའི་དངོས་པོའི་གཉེན་པོ་ཉིད་དུ་རྣམ་པར་སྒོམ་པར་བྱེད་དོ། ། ཞེས་སྒོམ་པའི་ལམ་ལ་འདོམས་པ་སྟེ། ཞེས་གསུངས།**
>
> nevertheless, by the nature of dependent arising (རྟེན་ཅིང་འབྲེལ་བར་འབྱུང་བའི་ཆོས་ཉིད), it is cultivated as the very antidote to the things it is to abandon.' So it instructs on the path of meditation." Thus [Haribhadra] says.

**On the source and its readings.** The sentence is Haribhadra's, from the འགྲེལ་པ་དོན་གསལ།, and it is reproduced almost letter for letter. The Tengyur text reads [མཐོང་བ་དང་བསྒོམ་པ་དག་ཐ་དད་དུ་གྱུར་པ་མེད་པས་བསྒོམ་པའི་ལམ་མཚན་ཉིད་པ་རྣམ་པར་བཞག་པ་ནི་མེད་ཀྱི། འོན་ཀྱང་དེ་ནི་རྟེན་ཅིང་འབྲེལ་པར་འབྱུང་བའི་ཆོས་ཉིད་ཀྱིས་…](quote:p424-q03). The yig cha writes སྒོམ where the Indian texts have བསྒོམ throughout. The Tengyur copy of the preceding clause reads ཐ་དད་པར་གདམས་པར་མི་ནུས་པ where the Commentaries copy and the yig cha have གདགས་པར; the reading is only reported here.

<!--line 519-692-->
> **དོན་ནི། རབ་འབྱོར་འདུས་བྱས་ཀྱི་དབྱིངས་ཞེས་སོགས་ཀྱི་མདོ་ཆོས་ཅན། སྒོམ་ལམ་ལ་གདམས་པ་ཡིན་ཏེ། དེ་ཡང་སྤང་བྱ་ལྷན་སྐྱེས་ཀྱི་གཉེན་པོར་སྒོམ་པའི་ཚུལ་གྱིས་འདོམས་པའི་མདོ་ཚིག་ཡིན་པའི་ཕྱིར།**
>
> The meaning: the subject is the sūtra "Subhūti, the conditioned element (འདུས་བྱས་ཀྱི་དབྱིངས) …" and so on. It is an instruction on the path of meditation, because it is sūtra wording that instructs by way of cultivating [that path] as the antidote to the *innate* (ལྷན་སྐྱེས) objects of abandonment.

<!--line 692-762-->
> **དེ་ལ་ཡང་བསྒོམ་བྱ་ལམ་གྱི་རང་བཞིན་དང་། རྩོད་པ་སྤོང་བ་གཉིས་ལས། དང་པོ་ནི།**
>
> This has two parts: the nature of the path to be meditated, and dispelling an objection. The first:

<!--line 762-1009-->
> **ཆོས་ཉིད་མངོན་སུམ་དུ་རྟོགས་པའི་ཐེག་ཆེན་གྱི་སྒོམ་ལམ་དེ་ཆོས་ཉིད་མངོན་སུམ་དུ་རྟོགས་པའི་ཐེག་ཆེན་གྱི་མཐོང་ལམ་ལས་རིགས་ཐ་དད་པ་མ་ཡིན་ཏེ། དེ་འདྲའི་སྒོམ་ལམ་དེ་འདྲའི་མཐོང་ལམ་གྱིས་མངོན་སུམ་དུ་རྟོགས་པའི་མཚན་ཉིད་རྟོགས་ཚུལ་མཐུན་པར་དམིགས་ནས་སྒོམ་པར་བྱེད་པའི་ཕྱིར།**
>
> A Mahāyāna path of meditation that directly realizes reality (ཆོས་ཉིད་མངོན་སུམ་དུ་རྟོགས་པ) is not different in type (རིགས་ཐ་དད་པ་མ་ཡིན) from a Mahāyāna path of seeing that directly realizes reality. The reason is that such a path of meditation meditates by focusing, in a concordant mode of realization (རྟོགས་ཚུལ་མཐུན་པར), on the very character that such a path of seeing directly realized.

<!--line 1009-1160-->
> **གཉིས་པ་ལ། འོ་ན། ཐེག་ཆེན་གྱི་མཐོང་ལམ་དང་། ཐེག་ཆེན་གྱི་སྒོམ་ལམ་གཉིས་ལ་སྤང་བྱ་སྤོང་བའི་ནུས་པ་ལ་ཁྱད་པར་མེད་པར་ཐལ། དེ་གཉིས་རིགས་ཐ་དད་མ་ཡིན་པའི་ཕྱིར། ཟེར་ན།**
>
> Second [dispelling the objection]. Someone says: "Then it follows that the Mahāyāna path of seeing and the Mahāyāna path of meditation do not differ in their power to abandon what is to be abandoned, because the two are not different in type."

<!--line 1160-1265-->
> **དེ་ལ་ཁྱབ་པ་མེད་དེ། དེ་གཉིས་རིགས་ཐ་དད་མ་ཡིན་ཡང་ཐེག་ཆེན་གྱི་མཐོང་ལམ་དེས་ཤེས་སྒྲིབ་ལྷན་སྐྱེས་སྤོང་མི་ནུས་ལ།**
>
> There is no pervasion. Although the two are not different in type, the Mahāyāna path of seeing cannot abandon the innate obscurations to omniscience (ཤེས་སྒྲིབ་ལྷན་སྐྱེས),

<!--line 1265-1434-->
> **ཆོས་ཉིད་མངོན་སུམ་དུ་རྟོགས་པའི་ཐེག་ཆེན་གྱི་སྒོམ་ལམ་གྱིས་སྔ་མ་ལས་སྒོམ་པ་ཆེས་བསྲིངས་པའི་དབང་གིས་ཤེས་སྒྲིབ་ལྷན་སྐྱེས་སྤོང་ནུས་པ་དེ་རྟེན་འབྲེལ་གྱི་ཆོས་ཉིད་ཀྱིས་ཐོབ་པའི་ཕྱིར།**
>
> whereas the Mahāyāna path of meditation that directly realizes reality can abandon them, because its meditation has been prolonged far beyond the former (སྔ་མ་ལས་སྒོམ་པ་ཆེས་བསྲིངས་པའི་དབང་གིས). That it gains this ability comes about through the nature of dependent arising (རྟེན་འབྲེལ་གྱི་ཆོས་ཉིད).

<!--line 1434-1503-->
> **དེ་ལྟར་ན་བྱང་ཆུབ་ཀྱི་སེམས་དང་དེས་འཕངས་པའི་ཕྱིར། ཞེས་སོགས་ཀྱི་དོན་ནི།**
>
> As for the meaning of [Haribhadra's] "Thus, the mind of enlightenment and what is propelled by it …" and so on:

**On the lemma as printed.** Haribhadra's sentence goes on དེས་འཕངས་པའི་ཆོས་ཀྱི་ངོ་བོ་ཉིད་ཀྱི་ཤེས་རབ་ཀྱི་ཕ་རོལ་ཏུ་ཕྱིན་པ, and he has no ཕྱིར at that point. The ཕྱིར here belongs to the yig cha's abbreviated citation of the lemma, and the ཞེས་སོགས marks the cut. The Sungbum print of this text has the same wording here. Two clauses earlier it has two readings of its own: ཆེས་?་སྤངས་པའི, damaged, where this edition has ཆེས་བསྲིངས་པའི, and སྒྲིབ་ལྷན་སྐྱེས without ཤེས at the second mention. They are reported only as readings.

> Someone asks: if you explain [the root text as saying] "the instructions are to be known as having ten aspects," what is the reason the subject matter of the instructions is fixed at exactly ten?

>
> And the third [category, i.e. the favourable causes] too is fixed [as three]: the five eyes (སྤྱན་ལྔ), the cause of not depending on others (གཞན་གྱི་དྲིང་མི་འཇོག་པ) in putting practice into effect; the six clairvoyances (མངོན་ཤེས་དྲུག), the cause of practice being completed swiftly; and the instructions on the path of seeing and the path of meditation, the causes of practice reaching its end.

**On "third" and the arithmetic.** The paragraph calls the favourable causes "the third" (གསུམ་པ). This counts the adverse causes as one, their three members, and then this. The literal wording is loose, but the tally is not. Nature, object, basis, three adverse causes and three favourable causes make nine headings. The last of these counts twice, because the root verse lists མཐོང་ལམ་དང་། and བསྒོམ་ཞེས་བྱ separately. That is how nine headings yield ten instructions.

<!--line 2466-2580-->
> **ཞེས་འཆད་པ་ལ། དེས་འཕངས་པའི་ཆོས་ཀྱི་ངོ་བོ་ཉིད་ཤེས་རབ་ཀྱི་ཕ་རོལ་ཏུ་ཕྱིན་པ་ལ་སྒྲུབ་པའི་རྣམ་པ་གང་ཡིན་པ་མི་དམིགས་པ་དང་།**
>
> To explain this, [Haribhadra wrote]: "… of the perfection of wisdom, which is the nature of the dharmas propelled by that [mind of enlightenment], the aspect of practice is non-apprehension (མི་དམིགས་པ);

<!--line 2580-2822-->
> **དེའི་དམིགས་པ་གང་ཡིན་པ་འཕགས་པའི་བདེན་པ་བཞི་དང་། རྟེན་གང་ཡིན་པ་སྐྱབས་གསུམ་དང་། ཁྱད་པར་དུ་འགྲོ་བའི་རྒྱུ་གང་ཡིན་པ་མ་ཆགས་པ་དང་། མི་ལྡོག་པར་འགྲོ་བའི་རྒྱུ་གང་ཡིན་པ་ཡོངས་སུ་མི་ངལ་བ་དང་། ཐེག་པ་གཞན་གྱིས་མི་འགྲོ་བའི་རྒྱུ་གང་ཡིན་པ་ལམ་ཡོངས་སུ་འཛིན་པ་དང་།**
>
> its object, the four noble truths; its basis, the three refuges; the cause of its advancing ever higher, non-attachment; the cause of its not turning back, non-weariness; the cause of its not going by another vehicle, full grasp of the path;

> **གཞན་གྱི་དྲིང་མི་འཇོག་པར་འགྲོ་བ་ཉིད་ཀྱི་རྒྱུ་གང་ཡིན་པ་སྤྱན་ལྔ་དང་། རྣམ་པ་ཐམས་ཅད་མཁྱེན་པ་ཉིད་ཡོངས་སུ་རྫོགས་པའི་རྒྱུ་གང་ཡིན་པ་མངོན་པར་ཤེས་པ་དྲུག་དང་། མཐར་ཐུག་པའི་རྒྱུ་གང་དག་ཡིན་པ་མཐོང་བ་དང་སྒོམ་པའི་ལམ་དག་སྟེ།**
>
> the cause of going on without depending on others, the five eyes; the cause of completing the knowledge of all aspects, the six clairvoyances; and the causes of reaching the end, the paths of seeing and meditation —

>
> all of these are taught in the context of the instructions. Because by these alone every aim is completely fulfilled, the instructions are of ten kinds." These are the words of the text that [Haribhadra] spoke.

## 3 · Structural Context (Dkar Chag)

- **Abhisamayālaṃkāra location:** The first chapter, second of its ten topics: the instructions (གདམས་ངག). The pack announces no lemma for this paragraph's outline path, but the paragraph names its lemmas itself, and they are the last words of the two verses on the ten instructions: [སྒྲུབ་དང་བདེན་པ་རྣམས་དང་ནི། ། སངས་རྒྱས་ལ་སོགས་དཀོན་མཆོག་གསུམ། ། … མཐོང་ལམ་དང་། ། བསྒོམ་ཞེས་བྱ་ལ་གདམས་ངག་ནི། ། བཅུ་ཡི་བདག་ཉིད་ཤེས་པར་བྱ། །](quote:p424-q01). The first half of the paragraph comments on བསྒོམ་ཞེས་བྱ་ལ, the tenth item. The second half comments on the count in the last line. The root verse's own sequence (practice, truths, Jewels, non-attachment, non-weariness, grasp of the path, eyes, clairvoyances, seeing, meditation) is exactly the sequence Chökyi Gyaltsen groups under his four headings. His scheme is a grouping of the verse's list, not a reordering of it.
- **Sūtra location:** The sūtra the paragraph cites as རབ་འབྱོར་འདུས་བྱས་ཀྱི་དབྱིངས་ཞེས་སོགས is in the Kangyur Pañcaviṃśati: [དེ་ཅིའི་ཕྱིར་ཞེ་ན། འདུས་བྱས་ཀྱི་དབྱིངས་མ་ཡིན་པར་ཡང་འདུས་མ་བྱས་ཀྱི་དབྱིངས་གདགས་པར་མི་ནུས་སོ། །…](quote:p424-q02). There it is introduced with དེ་ཅིའི་ཕྱིར་ཞེ་ན, as the *reason* for the preceding statement that the conditioned and unconditioned elements do not "see" each other. The Kangyur text reads མ་ཡིན་པར་ཡང where p423 and the Gelug commentators have མེད་པར. The meaning is not affected.
- **Haribhadra's commentary location:** The first half of the paragraph quotes Haribhadra's gloss on བསྒོམ་ཞེས་བྱ whole ([q03](quote:p424-q03)). The second half quotes his closing summary of the ten, which ends [དེ་དག་ཐམས་ཅད་གདམས་ངག་གི་སྐབས་སུ་བསྟན་ཏེ། འདི་ཙམ་ཁོ་ནས་དོན་ཐམས་ཅད་ཕུན་སུམ་ཚོགས་པར་འགྱུར་བས་གདམས་ངག་ནི་རྣམ་པ་བཅུའོ། །](quote:p424-q04). Directly after that line the འགྲེལ་པ་དོན་གསལ། goes on to the twenty saṅgha. On reading the aligned place, **two things are absent from Haribhadra.** (a) He raises no objection about the power to abandon. His འོན་ཀྱང clause asserts that the path of meditation is an antidote "by the nature of dependent arising", but he does not set up the "then there would be no difference in abandoning power" objection. He also does not say which obscurations the path of meditation removes; "innate" and "cognitive obscuration" are not in his text here. (b) He gives no argument for the count. His "by these alone every aim is fulfilled" is a statement of sufficiency, and the fourfold nature/object/basis/cause division is not in him. Both the objection-and-answer and the fourfold scheme are Tibetan additions. The first is Gyaltsab Je's arrangement, the second Tsongkhapa's (see §4).
- **Text location:** སྐབས་དང་པོའི་སྤྱི་དོན།, under སྒོམ་ལམ་ལ་གདམས་པ། (opened at p423), in the second division ༢༽ བསྟན་བཅོས་ཀྱིས་འགྲེལ་ཚུལ. That division splits into ༡༽ བསྒོམ་བྱ་ལམ་གྱི་རང་བཞིན། and ༢༽ རྩོད་པ་སྤོང་པ, both of which open and close inside this paragraph. The pack's outline has no separate heading for the section on why the count is ten (གྲངས་ངེས). It sits after the dispelling of the objection with no heading of its own, which is why the outline path shows only the two sub-headings. Whether the count discussion should be read as a third, unannounced division or as an appendix to the path-of-meditation topic is **unclear** from the outline.
- **Section type:** Commentarial exposition (quoting the Indian commentary, then restating it as a syllogism), followed by a short objection and response, followed by a question on the count answered positively.
- **Argumentative role:** It closes the treatment of the ten instructions, and with it the general meaning of the first chapter. The path-of-meditation half keeps the claim that the two paths are one in type while keeping the paths distinct in function. The count half turns Haribhadra's closing formula into an argument that nothing is missing from the list and nothing is superfluous.

## 4 · Philosophical Exposition & Commentary

**Core meaning — the path of meditation.** Haribhadra's sentence is startling if taken at its word: "no defined path of meditation is set up." Chökyi Gyaltsen does not take it at its word. He restates it as a claim about *type* (རིགས). A Mahāyāna path of meditation that directly realizes reality is not a different kind of cognition from a Mahāyāna path of seeing that directly realizes reality, because it takes the same reality as its object in the same way (རྟོགས་ཚུལ་མཐུན་པར). He adds the qualifier ཆོས་ཉིད་མངོན་སུམ་དུ་རྟོགས་པའི to *both* paths, and it is doing work. A Mahāyāna path of meditation in the post-equipoise period is still a path of meditation, yet it does not directly realize reality. Without the qualifier, the thesis would claim that such a cognition too is of the same type as the seeing path's direct realization, which nobody holds. So the qualifier restricts the thesis to the equipoise, which is where Haribhadra's "focuses on what is not different from the thing made manifest" applies.

**Where Chökyi Gyaltsen's wording comes from.** The two-heading structure and the key word are Gyaltsab Je's. The རྣམ་བཤད་སྙིང་པོའི་རྒྱན། divides the same passage into བསྒོམ་བྱ་ལམ་གྱི་རང་བཞིན་དང་། རྩོད་སྤང and writes [ཆོས་ཉིད་མངོན་སུམ་དུ་རྟོགས་པའི་མཐོང་ལམ་ལས་རིགས་ཐ་དད་པའི་ཟག་པ་མེད་པའི་སྒོམ་པའི་ལམ་མཚན་ཉིད་པ་རྣམ་པར་བཞག་པ་ནི་མེད་དོ། ། ཆོས་ཉིད་ལ་མངོན་སུམ་དུ་མཉམ་པར་བཞག་པའི་མཐོང་བ་དང་བསྒོམ་པ་དག་རིགས་ཐ་དད་དུ་གྱུར་པ་མེད་པས་སོ། །](quote:p424-q11). He reads Haribhadra's "no defined path of meditation" as "no path of meditation *different in type* from the seeing path." He also restricts it explicitly to cognitions "set in equipoise directly on reality." Chökyi Gyaltsen's ཆོས་ཉིད་མངོན་སུམ་དུ་རྟོགས་པའི is the same restriction put into the subject of the syllogism. This is dependence within one transmission, Gyaltsab Je being the source the yig cha follows most closely here.

Tsongkhapa's ལེགས་བཤད་གསེར་ཕྲེང reaches the same result by a different wording: [མཐོང་ལམ་གྱི་ཆོས་ཉིད་རྟོགས་ཚུལ་དང་མི་མཐུན་པའི་སྒོམ་ལམ་མཚན་ཉིད་པ་རྣམ་པར་བཞག་པ་ནི་མེད་ཀྱི་བཏགས་པ་བ་ཡིན་ཏེ།](quote:p424-q06). The criterion is the *mode of realizing reality*. Tsongkhapa's summary of the Indian position is that [མདོར་ན་སློབ་དཔོན་དང་འཕགས་པས་ས་བཅུའི་མཉམ་གཞག་གི་རྟོགས་ཚུལ་ལ་ཁྱད་མེད་པར་བཞེད་དོ། །](quote:p424-q19): Haribhadra and Vimuktisena alike hold that the equipoises of all ten grounds do not differ in how they realize. That is the underlying thesis. "Not different in type" and "not different in mode of realization" are two Gelug ways of stating it.

**The objection and its answer.** If the two paths are one in type, why can the one do what the other cannot? Chökyi Gyaltsen answers by denying the pervasion. Sameness of type does not entail sameness of power. The path of meditation abandons the innate cognitive obscurations "by the force of meditation prolonged far beyond the former," and that it gains this power is "by the nature of dependent arising." Gyaltsab Je is again the model, nearly clause for clause: [ལམ་སྔ་མས་ལྷན་སྐྱེས་ཀྱི་ས་བོན་མི་སྤོང་ཡང་། ལམ་ཕྱི་མ་དེ་ནི་སྔ་མ་ལས་གོམས་པ་ཆེས་བསྲིངས་པའི་དབང་གིས་རྟེན་ཅིང་འབྲེལ་བར་འབྱུང་བའི་ཆོས་ཉིད་ཀྱིས་ལྷན་སྐྱེས་ཀྱི་ས་བོན་སྤོང་ནུས་པའི་ཕྱིར། དཔེར་ན་སྒྲ་མི་རྟག་རྟོགས་ཀྱི་རྗེས་དཔག་ཚད་མས། …](quote:p424-q12). Gyaltsab Je adds an example that Chökyi Gyaltsen leaves out. An inference realizing sound as impermanent realizes that the object of the innate grasping at permanence does not exist, yet it harms only the imputed, manifest form of that grasping, not the innate form. Tsongkhapa's example is homelier. Earlier and later water are no different, yet the later washes clean what the earlier could not, and if asked why, one can only say [དངོས་པོ་རྣམས་ཀྱི་ཆོས་ཉིད་ཡིན་པས་སོ](quote:p424-q07), "it is the nature of things." The two examples point at different things. Gyaltsab Je's locates the difference in the *object's* depth: the same realization cuts the manifest form but not the innate one. Tsongkhapa's locates it in *accumulation*: more of the same does what less could not. Chökyi Gyaltsen's own wording, སྒོམ་པ་ཆེས་བསྲིངས་པ, sides with the second emphasis. He gives no example.

**Why "innate cognitive obscurations" in particular.** This is where Chökyi Gyaltsen departs in wording from all three of his predecessors. Gyaltsab Je says "the seeds of the innate" (ལྷན་སྐྱེས་ཀྱི་ས་བོན), Tsongkhapa "what is to be abandoned by meditation" (སྒོམ་སྤང), and Panchen Sonam Drakpa "the seeds of the innate objects of abandonment." Chökyi Gyaltsen alone names ཤེས་སྒྲིབ་ལྷན་སྐྱེས. The nearest support I located is Gyaltsab Je's own chapter-four definition of where the Mahāyāna path of seeing ends: [ཤེས་སྒྲིབ་ལྷན་སྐྱེས་ཀྱི་ས་བོན་དངོས་སུ་གཞོམ་པའི་ཆེད་དུ། སྟོང་པ་ཉིད་ལ་མངོན་སུམ་དུ་མཉམ་པར་མི་འཇོག་པ་དེ་སྲིད་ཚུན་ཆད་མཐོང་ལམ་གྱིས་བསྡུས་པར་ཤེས་པར་བྱའོ། །](quote:p424-q13). One remains on the path of seeing until one sets the mind in equipoise on emptiness in order actually to destroy the seeds of the innate cognitive obscurations. So the innate cognitive obscurations are the object whose engagement marks the boundary between the two Mahāyāna paths, and naming them is the cleanest counter-instance to the opponent's claim. Why Chökyi Gyaltsen does not also name the innate afflictive obscurations is not stated in this paragraph. One plausible reason is that a counter-instance needs only one obscuration. Another is that a bodhisattva who has come from the Hīnayāna has no innate afflictions left to abandon on the Mahāyāna path of meditation. Neither is stated here, and I did not locate a passage in his other textbooks that settles it. The point should be treated as **open**.

**Jetsun Chökyi Gyaltsen's own view elsewhere.** The same two-level structure appears in his chapter-four general meaning, where the question is whether a Mahāyāna path of meditation that ultimately neither increases nor decreases can do anything: [ཐེག་ཆེན་གྱི་སྒོམ་ལམ་དོན་དམ་པར་འཕེལ་འགྲིབ་མེད་ཀྱང་ཐ་སྙད་དུ་ཐེག་ཆེན་གྱི་སྒོམ་ལམ་གྱིས་སྤང་བྱ་ཉམས་པ་དང་། གཉེན་པོ་འཕེལ་བའི་བྱ་བ་བྱེད་པའི་ཕྱིར།](quote:p424-q16). This gives the best reading of what "the nature of dependent arising" does in the present paragraph. It is not an appeal to mystery. It says that efficacy belongs to the conventional level, where causes and conditions operate, while the sameness of the two paths is a sameness in how they engage reality. The objection conflates the two levels, and the answer separates them.

**Core meaning — the count of ten.** Haribhadra asserts sufficiency and nothing more. The argument for exactly ten is Tsongkhapa's, and Chökyi Gyaltsen takes it over almost word for word: [སྤྱིར་གནས་སྐབས་དང་མཐར་ཐུག་གི་དོན་སྒྲུབ་པ་ལ་སྒྲུབ་པ་རང་གི་ངོ་བོ། དམིགས་ཡུལ། རྟེན། སྒྲུབ་པའི་རྒྱུ་སྟེ་བཞིར་ངེས་ལ་](quote:p424-q09). The three kinds of laziness (བྱ་བ་ངན་ཞེན, སྒྱིད་ལུག, ཞུམ་པ), matched to non-attachment, non-weariness and grasp of the path, are also in the གསེར་ཕྲེང. So is the triad of favourable causes: not depending on others, swift completion of the accumulations, and final abandonment. Tsongkhapa glosses the last pair more precisely than the yig cha does: [མཐོང་སྤང་མཐར་ཐུག་པ་སྟེ་མ་ལུས་པར་སྤོང་བའི་རྒྱུ་མཐོང་ལམ་དང་སྒོམ་སྤང་མཐར་ཐུག་པར་སྤོང་བའི་རྒྱུ་སྒོམ་ལམ](quote:p424-q10). The path of seeing is the cause of the final abandonment of what is to be abandoned by seeing, and the path of meditation of what is to be abandoned by meditation. Chökyi Gyaltsen's "causes of practice reaching its end" is the compressed form of that.

**An objection the count invites.** If these ten alone fulfil every aim, the rest of the *Ornament* would seem superfluous. Gyaltsab Je answers this in advance: [འོག་ནས་འཆད་པའི་ལམ་གཞན་དགོས་པ་མེད་པར་འགྱུར་རོ་སྙམ་དུ་མི་གཟུང་སྟེ། འདིར་མ་འདུས་པའི་ཐེག་པ་ཆེན་པོའི་ལམ་མི་སྲིད་པའི་ཕྱིར། འོན་ཀྱང་གསལ་བའི་དབྱེ་བ་མཐའ་དག་འདིར་མ་བསྟན་པས་གཞན་ལས་རྟོགས་དགོས་པའི་ཕྱིར་རོ། །](quote:p424-q14). No Mahāyāna path falls outside these ten, but their particular divisions are not all shown here and must be learned elsewhere. Chökyi Gyaltsen does not raise this. His "every aim of a person's becoming a buddha" (གང་ཟག་གཅིག་འཚང་རྒྱ་བའི་བཞེད་དོན) is Tsongkhapa's phrase, not Gyaltsab Je's "every aim of one seeking omniscience," and he leaves the sufficiency claim unqualified.

**Sera Jey specificity and Loseling.** Panchen Sonam Drakpa's འགྲེལ་ཚིག་དོན་རབ་གསལ། shows the other Gelug option clearly: [མཐོང་ལམ་གྱིས་རྟོགས་པའི་ཆོས་ཉིད་དང་མི་འདྲ་བའི་ཆོས་ཉིད་རྟོགས་པའི་སྒོམ་ལམ་མཚན་ཉིད་པ་རྣམ་པར་བཞག་པ་ནི་མེད་དེ།](quote:p424-q15). He follows Tsongkhapa's "mode of realizing reality" wording, framed in terms of the *reality realized*, and he follows Tsongkhapa in the objection too (ཆོས་ཉིད་རྟོགས་ཚུལ་ལ་ཁྱད་པར་མེད་མོད). Chökyi Gyaltsen follows Gyaltsab Je's *type* (རིགས). The substance is the same in both. The Sera Jey text, though, casts the thesis as a claim about the paths' kind, and puts the object and the mode of realization into the reason (མཚན་ཉིད་རྟོགས་ཚུལ་མཐུན་པར་དམིགས་ནས). That arrangement makes the objection sharper. It is precisely "same type" from which the opponent tries to infer "same power."

## 5 · Bones of Contention 🦴

**What is disputed?** Three things. (1) How to read Haribhadra's "no defined path of meditation" — whether it denies that a real path of meditation exists at all. (2) What "conditioned" and "unconditioned" stand for in the sūtra's example. (3) Whether the ten-grounds equipoises differ in any respect of what they see.

**Who argues what?**

- **Ārya Vimuktisena**, Haribhadra's own source, in the ཉི་ཁྲི་སྣང་བ།: [དེ་ལ་འདུས་བྱས་ནི་ལམ་མོ། ། འདུས་མ་བྱས་ནི་སྤངས་པ་སྟེ། དེ་གཉིས་ཕན་ཚུན་ཐ་དད་པར་གདགས་པར་མི་ནུས་པ་ཉིད་ཀྱིས་ན་སྒོམ་པའི་ལམ་རྣམ་པར་གཞག་པ་ནི་བཏགས་པ་ཡིན་གྱི། མཚན་ཉིད་པ་ནི་མ་ཡིན་ཏེ།](quote:p424-q05). Taken literally, the setting-up of the path of meditation is *nominal* (བཏགས་པ) and not *defined* (མཚན་ཉིད་པ). This is the reading that makes the Indian text sound as though there were no real path of meditation. It would be intolerable to a system that counts the path of meditation among the five paths and gives it nine grades of abandonment.
- **Tsongkhapa** keeps Vimuktisena's word བཏགས་པ་བ but confines it to one respect: there is no path of meditation "whose mode of realizing reality does not accord with the seeing path's," and in *that* respect a separate path of meditation is only nominal ([q06](quote:p424-q06)). **Panchen Sonam Drakpa** follows him ([q15](quote:p424-q15)). **Gyaltsab Je** drops the word "nominal" altogether and substitutes "different in type" ([q11](quote:p424-q11)). **Chökyi Gyaltsen** follows Gyaltsab Je and never uses བཏགས་པ. His thesis says what the path of meditation *is* (the same type as the seeing path) rather than what it is *not* (a defined path). This is a real difference of emphasis within the Gelug commentaries. It is not a disagreement of doctrine, because all four hold that the path of meditation exists and that the two paths differ in what they abandon. The Indian source, by contrast, does not state that restriction.
- **On the example**, Tsongkhapa records and rejects a reading by Suvarṇadvīpa: [འདིར་གསེར་གླིང་པས་འདུས་བྱས་ཟག་བཅས་ཀྱི་གཟུགས་སོགས་དང་། འདུས་མ་བྱས་ཟག་མེད་ཀྱི་ཚུལ་ཁྲིམས་ལ་སོགས་པ་ལ་འཆད་པ་ནི། འཕགས་པས་འདུས་བྱས་ནི་ལམ་མོ། ། འདུས་མ་བྱས་ནི་སྤངས་པའོ་ཞེས་གསུངས་པ་མ་མཐོང་བ](quote:p424-q08). Suvarṇadvīpa took "conditioned" as contaminated form and the like and "unconditioned" as uncontaminated ethics and the like. Tsongkhapa says this ignores Vimuktisena's gloss (conditioned = path, unconditioned = abandonment), which Haribhadra adopts. Tsongkhapa reads the example as the path-truth and the cessation-truth (ལམ་བདེན / འགོག་བདེན). Gyaltsab Je similarly reads it as the path-truth that directly realizes selflessness and the unconditioned that is free from the seeds of the stains. The yig cha does not unpack the example at all, and simply quotes Haribhadra.
- **On the ten grounds**, Gyaltsab Je names an earlier Tibetan position at exactly this place: [སྐབས་འདིར་སྔ་མ་ཁ་ཅིག་གིས། ས་བཅུའི་མཉམ་གཞག་ལ་ཆོས་ཉིད་མཐོང་བ་ལ་ཁྱད་པར་མེད་ཀྱང་། ཆོས་སྐུ་མཐོང་བ་ལ་ཁྱད་པར་ཡོད་དོ་ཞེས་ཟེར་བ་ནི།](quote:p424-q18). On this view the equipoises of the ten grounds do not differ in seeing reality, but they do differ in seeing the dharmakāya. He grants that the view would be coherent for someone who holds the truth of cessation to be an ultimate truth. But if what is meant is a conventional truth, it is beyond reason for a learner's wisdom that directly realizes reality to be directly cognizing a conventional truth. That would imply that a learner's equipoise and subsequent attainment could become one entity, and that the stain of apprehending the two truths as different entities had been exhausted. Chökyi Gyaltsen does not revisit this. His "concordant mode of realization" is compatible only with Gyaltsab Je's side of it.
- **On the count**, Tsongkhapa records a ཁ་ཅིག who takes "the cause of practice reaching its end" as mundane and supramundane practice, rather than as the paths of seeing and meditation ([q10](quote:p424-q10)). The yig cha takes Tsongkhapa's reading without mentioning the alternative.

**Why does it matter?** Because the path of meditation's relation to the path of seeing is the test case for a central Prajñāpāramitā claim: that the direct realization of emptiness is one realization, the same on the first ground and the tenth. If the two paths differed in what they see, emptiness would have grades. If they differed in nothing, there would be no path of meditation to cultivate, and the root verse's tenth instruction would be empty. The paragraph holds both positions at once. The paths are one in type and in object, and they differ in power through the length of habituation. The phrase "by the nature of dependent arising" is what keeps the two claims from colliding.

**Resolution.** Sera Jey follows Gyaltsab Je. The thesis is stated as sameness of type among cognitions that directly realize reality, and the objection from sameness of type to sameness of power is refused for lack of pervasion. The difference in power is located in prolonged meditation and grounded conventionally in dependent arising. The ground is not a difference in the reality realized. The Indian commentaries' language of a "nominal" path of meditation is not taken up. The evidence for this position is strong and close to the curriculum, since Gyaltsab Je and Tsongkhapa both treat the point at the aligned place. The one element I could not trace to a predecessor is the specification of the *cognitive* obscurations. That element should be read as Chökyi Gyaltsen's own precision, supported indirectly by Gyaltsab Je's chapter-four boundary, and not as an inherited formula.

## 6 · Key Terminology & Technical Concepts

- **སྒོམ་པའི་ལམ་མཚན་ཉིད་པ།** — a "defined" or "actual" path of meditation, as opposed to one so called only nominally (བཏགས་པ་བ).
  - **How understood here:** Not denied outright. What is denied is a path of meditation with its own *kind* of realization, set apart from the seeing path. The paragraph's own reformulation makes this explicit.
  - **Debates:** Vimuktisena's "nominal, not defined" versus Tsongkhapa's restriction to the mode of realization and Gyaltsab Je's "different in type"; see §5.

- **རིགས་ཐ་དད།** — difference of type or kind between two cognitions.
  - **How understood here:** The Gyaltsab Je–Chökyi Gyaltsen criterion. Two cognitions that take the same object in the same mode (the same "character," the same way of realizing it) are the same in type, whatever else distinguishes them. The objection tries to extend "same type" to "same power," and the answer is that type does not settle power.
  - **Debates:** Tsongkhapa and Panchen Sonam Drakpa put the same point as "no difference in mode of realizing reality" (ཆོས་ཉིད་རྟོགས་ཚུལ).

- **ཆོས་ཉིད་མངོན་སུམ་དུ་རྟོགས་པའི།** — "that directly realizes reality," the qualifier on both paths.
  - **How understood here:** It confines the sameness claim to equipoise. Post-equipoise states of the path of meditation are paths of meditation but do not realize reality directly, and the thesis does not cover them. Gyaltsab Je's ཆོས་ཉིད་ལ་མངོན་སུམ་དུ་མཉམ་པར་བཞག་པ does the same work.

- **ཤེས་སྒྲིབ་ལྷན་སྐྱེས།** — the innate obscurations to omniscience: the cognitive obscurations that are present by nature rather than instilled by philosophical tenets.
  - **Sanskrit equivalent:** *sahaja jñeyāvaraṇa*.
  - **How understood here:** The paragraph's instance of what the Mahāyāna path of meditation can abandon and the path of seeing cannot. It contrasts with the imputed (ཀུན་བཏགས) objects of abandonment that p423 assigned to the path of seeing.
  - **Debates:** The predecessors say "innate [seeds]" or "what is abandoned by meditation" without restricting it to the cognitive obscurations; see §4 on why the restriction is left open.

- **རྟེན་ཅིང་འབྲེལ་བར་འབྱུང་བའི་ཆོས་ཉིད།** — "the nature of dependent arising": the way things are, whereby a cause brings about its result.
  - **How understood here:** Not a gesture at the inexplicable, but the conventional ground of efficacy. Tsongkhapa's water example and Chökyi Gyaltsen's own chapter-four statement that the path of meditation acts "conventionally" (ཐ་སྙད་དུ) while ultimately without increase or decrease both show this. It lets two paths be one in what they see and different in what they do.

- **གྲངས་ངེས།** — definiteness of number: the demonstration that a list has exactly the members it has.
  - **How understood here:** Achieved by nesting. Four heads (nature, object, basis, causes), with the causes split two ways (adverse and favourable) and each of those three ways. The final member is doubled, because the root verse names seeing and meditation separately.
  - **Debates:** Haribhadra asserts sufficiency without an argument for the number. The argument is Tsongkhapa's, and Gyaltsab Je adds the caveat that sufficiency does not make the later chapters redundant.

- **གཞན་གྱི་དྲིང་མི་འཇོག་པ།** — not depending on (literally "not waiting on") others.
  - **How understood here:** The function assigned to the five eyes. Having one's own sight of what to adopt and reject, one does not need another's guidance to practise. Tsongkhapa: སྤང་བླང་རང་གིས་ཤེས་པས.
