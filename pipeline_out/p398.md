# Are the Three Jewels complete in the final refuge? — the Saṅgha's culmination, and the two refuges defined

## 1 · Passage & Context

**Source Text:**

> འོ་ན་མཐར་ཐུག་གི་སྐྱབས་ལ་དཀོན་མཆོག་གསུམ་མ་ཚང་བར་འགྱུར་རོ། ། ཞེ་ན༑ དེ་ལ་དེ་གསུམ་ཚང་སྟེ། སྐྱབས་གསུམ་གྱི་ཟླས་ཕྱེ་བའི་ཚོགས་དགེ་འདུན་དེ་ཡང་སངས་རྒྱས་ཐོབ་པ་ན་སྐྱབས་དེའི་མཐར་ཐུག་པར་འགྱུར་བའི་ཕྱིར་ཏེ། དེ་ཉིད་ལས༑ ཚོགས་ཀྱང་དེ་ཡི་མཐར་ཐུག་ཕྱིར། ། ཞེས་དང་། གསེར་ཕྲེང་ལས། ཚོགས་ཀྱང་དེ་ཡི་མཐར་ཐུག་ཕྱིར། ། ཞེས་མཐར་ཐུག་གི་སྐྱབས་ལ་དཀོན་མཆོག་གསུམ་མ་ཚང་ངམ་སྙམ་པའི་ལོག་རྟོག་གཅད་པའི་ཕྱིར་དུ་གསུངས། ཞེས་གསུངས་པའི་ཕྱིར། དེས་ན་ཁྱོད་ཡིན་པའམ་ཁྱོད་ཀྱི་ནང་ཚན་དུ་གྱུར་པའི་དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས་མེད་པ་གང་ཞིག ། དངོས་པོའི་གནས་ཚོད་ལ་ལམ་གྱི་བགྲོད་པ་མཐར་མ་ཐུག་པའི་སྐྱབས། ཀུན་རྫོབ་པའི་སྐྱབས་ཀྱི་མཚན་ཉིད། དེ་དང་གནས་སྐབས་ཀྱི་སྐྱབས་དོན་གཅིག ། དངོས་པོའི་གནས་ཚོད་ལ་ལམ་གྱི་བགྲོད་པ་མཐར་ཐུག་པའི་སྐྱབས། དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས་ཀྱི་མཚན་ཉིད། དེ་དང་མཐར་ཐུག་གི་སྐྱབས་དོན་གཅིག །

**Preceding Section:** p397 opens the second sub-heading, the ultimate refuge (༢༽ དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས). Asked what the ultimate refuge is, Chökyi Gyaltsen answers that the Buddha alone is the final refuge, because the Buddha has as his basis the Dharma that has perfected the qualities of the two truths (བདེན་གཉིས་ཀྱི་ཡོན་ཏན་མཐར་ཕྱིན་པའི་ཆོས་ཀྱི་རྟེན་ཅན). His proof text is the first three lines of the Uttaratantra verse, དམ་པའི་དོན་དུ་འགྲོ་བ་ཡི། ། སྐྱབས་ནི་སངས་རྒྱས་ཉག་གཅིག་ཡིན། ། ཐུབ་པ་ཆོས་ཀྱི་སྐུ་ཅན་ཕྱིར༑. He stops before the fourth line. Before that, under the first sub-heading, he had argued that the Dharma of scripture, the learners' path, the nirvāṇa without remainder, and the tenth-ground bodhisattvas and Hīnayāna arhats are not final refuges, drawing on Asaṅga's commentary on the preceding verse.

**Following Section:** p399 turns to a named opponent. The author of the Rong ṭīk (རོང་ཊཱིཀ་པ) holds that none of these is the final refuge: the Dharma Jewel of scripture, because it is abandoned like a raft once realization is complete; the learner's path truth, because it is false and deceptive; the learner's cessation truth, because it is a non-affirming negation; and the learner ārya, because he still fears the obstructions to omniscience. Chökyi Gyaltsen answers with consequences. If being scriptural Dharma meant not being a final refuge, the Buddha's own speech, included in his continuum, would not be one either. And if the Rong ṭīk's reason for the learner's path held, the two form bodies, which are also compounded, would not be final refuges. He cites the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā's འདུས་བྱས་ཐམས་ཅད་སླུ་བའི་ཆོས།.

**What this paragraph is doing.** It does two things. First it answers the obvious objection to p397. If the Buddha *alone* is the final refuge, then the final refuge seems to lack the Dharma and the Saṅgha, so the Three Jewels would not be complete in it. The answer is that they are complete, because the Saṅgha comes to its culmination in Buddhahood. The fourth line of the same Uttaratantra verse is cited for this, with a report that the ལེགས་བཤད་གསེར་ཕྲེང cites it for exactly this purpose. Second, it draws the conclusion (དེས་ན) of the whole heading: the formal definitions of conventional and ultimate refuge, with the equivalences conventional = provisional (གནས་སྐབས) and ultimate = final (མཐར་ཐུག). The definitions are the tool p399 uses against the Rong ṭīk.

>
> A refuge whose traversal of the path has reached its end as the thing actually stands is the definition of an ultimate refuge (དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས).

<!--line 679-713-->
> **དེ་དང་མཐར་ཐུག་གི་སྐྱབས་དོན་གཅིག །**
>
> It is equivalent to the final refuge.

**On the citation from the Golden Rosary.** Read naturally, everything between གསེར་ཕྲེང་ལས and the second ཞེས་གསུངས is presented as the Golden Rosary's words: the verse line plus a statement of why it was spoken. I did not find those words in Tsongkhapa's text. The phrase ལོག་རྟོག་གཅད་པའི་ཕྱིར་དུ་གསུངས occurs in this archive only in this yig cha, and the Sungbum copies of the same work repeat it. It does not occur in the ལེགས་བཤད་གསེར་ཕྲེང as held here, nor in the Tsongkhapa father-and-sons collection. What the Golden Rosary *does* contain is the qualm and its answer in its own words (see §4). The clause is therefore best read as Chökyi Gyaltsen's statement of the Golden Rosary's purpose in citing the line, not as a verbatim quotation. Another recension of the Golden Rosary could, of course, read differently.

## 3 · Structural Context (Dkar Chag)

- **Abhisamayālaṃkāra location:** the ten instructions (གདམས་ངག་བཅུ) of the first chapter, at the second item: [སྒྲུབ་དང་བདེན་པ་རྣམས་དང་ནི། ། སངས་རྒྱས་ལ་སོགས་དཀོན་མཆོག་གསུམ། ། མ་ཞེན་ཡོངས་སུ་མི་ངལ་དང་། །](quote:p398-q06). The root text says nothing more than "the Three Jewels, the Buddha and the rest." The refuge excursus as a whole, of which this is one paragraph, hangs from those five syllables.
- **Haribhadra's commentary location:** the འགྲེལ་པ་དོན་གསལ། at the same lemma. Haribhadra explains the instruction on the Three Jewels as instruction to engage them without apprehending anything. On the Saṅgha, he specifies who is being instructed: [དགེ་འདུན་ལ་སངས་རྒྱས་དཀོན་མཆོག་གི་ཁོངས་སུ་གཏོགས་པ་ཉིད་ཀྱི་དགྲ་བཅོམ་པ་མ་གཏོགས་པ་འབྲས་བུ་ལ་གནས་པ་དང་ཞུགས་པའི་བྱེ་བྲག་གིས་སྐྱེས་བུ་ཆེན་པོ](quote:p398-q05). The twenty members of the Saṅgha exclude "the arhat who is included within the Buddha Jewel." That is the only point at which Haribhadra comes near this paragraph's question. He does not distinguish conventional from ultimate refuge. He does not cite the Ratnagotravibhāga, and he never asks whether the Three Jewels are complete in the final refuge. The whole apparatus of this sub-heading comes into the Prajñāpāramitā curriculum from the Uttaratantra, by way of Gyaltsab Je and Tsongkhapa. It does not come from the Indian commentary on the Ornament. There is one link, and it is not trivial. Haribhadra's phrase "the arhat included within the Buddha Jewel" is exactly the text an opponent used at p386 to argue that the Buddha is not the Saṅgha Jewel. Chökyi Gyaltsen's reply there, discussed in §4, is what makes this paragraph's answer possible.
- **Text location:** in this text's outline, the section on the instruction regarding the three refuges (སྐྱབས་གསུམ་ལ་གདམས་པ།, opening at p376) has a third part: the difference between conventional and ultimate refuge (གསུམ་པ་ཀུན་རྫོབ་པའི་སྐྱབས་དང་དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས་ཀྱི་ཁྱད་པར་བཤད་པ, opening at p396). Its second sub-heading, the ultimate refuge (༢༽ དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས), opens at p397. This paragraph is the second of that sub-heading. Notice that the definitions of *both* refuges are placed under the heading for the ultimate one. They are the conclusion (དེས་ན) of the two sub-headings taken together, not a gloss on the second alone. The pack gives no new heading at p399, so the debate with the Rong ṭīk appears to fall under the same sub-heading, as a dispute appended to it. I have not been able to confirm this from the outline.
- **Section type:** own-system exposition (རང་ལུགས). It combines an objection and reply (འོ་ན … ཞེ་ན) with a closing set of definitions.
- **Argumentative role:** it secures p397's claim against its most natural misreading, that "the Buddha alone" excludes the Dharma and the Saṅgha. It then turns the classification of the two refuges into definitions exact enough to test cases. That is what p399 does with them: every instance the Rong ṭīk offers is measured against "has the traversal of the path reached its end."

## 4 · Philosophical Exposition & Commentary

**The canonical source.** The line quoted twice in this paragraph is the fourth pāda of a verse in Maitreya's Ratnagotravibhāga. In the Tengyur it reads [དམ་པའི་དོན་དུ་འགྲོ་བ་ཡི། །སྐྱབས་ནི་སངས་རྒྱས་ཉག་གཅིག་ཡིན། །ཐུབ་པ་ཆོས་ཀྱི་སྐུ་ཅན་ཕྱིར། །ཚོགས་ཀྱང་དེ་ཡི་མཐར་ཐུག་ཕྱིར། །](quote:p398-q01). It comes directly after the verse that denies final-refuge status to the two kinds of Dharma and the ārya assembly (སྤང་ཕྱིར་བསླུ་བའི་ཆོས་ཅན་ཕྱིར། …). That earlier verse is the text behind the first sub-heading and behind p399. The line is Maitreya's and belongs to the Uttaratantra, not to the Golden Rosary or this yig cha, both of which quote it. Asaṅga's Vyākhyā glosses it as follows: [ཐེག་པ་གསུམ་པོའི་ཚོགས་ཀྱང་ཆོས་ཀྱི་སྐུ་རྣམ་པར་དག་པ་མཐར་ཐུག་པ་ཐོབ་པས་མཐར་ཕྱིན་པར་འགྱུར་བའི་ཕྱིར་ན།](quote:p398-q02), "because the assembly of the three vehicles, too, reaches its completion by attaining the utterly pure, final dharmakāya." The yig cha's reason, སངས་རྒྱས་ཐོབ་པ་ན་སྐྱབས་དེའི་མཐར་ཐུག་པར་འགྱུར་བ, is a close paraphrase of Asaṅga, not of Tsongkhapa.

**Core meaning.** The worry is structural. p397 made the Buddha *alone* the final refuge. Yet a refuge in this tradition is threefold, and the ultimate refuge is supposed to be the ultimate version of what one goes for refuge to. If only one of the three is final, then two of the three have no final form. The reply denies that consequence without retreating from "the Buddha alone." The Saṅgha is not a second final refuge alongside the Buddha. It *ends in* the Buddha: a Saṅgha member who completes the path becomes a Buddha, and the Saṅgha's own final state is Buddhahood. So the final refuge, which is one thing, is still complete as three.

**The premise the paragraph does not state.** This works only if a Buddha is himself a member of the Saṅgha Jewel. Otherwise the Saṅgha would culminate in something outside itself, and the final refuge would contain no Saṅgha at all. Chökyi Gyaltsen has already committed himself on exactly this, a dozen paragraphs earlier (p386), against an opponent arguing from Haribhadra's "arhat included within the Buddha Jewel": [དེ་ལྟར་མ་ཡིན་ན་མཐར་ཐུག་གི་དཀོན་མཆོག་གསུམ་མེད་པར་ཐལ། སངས་རྒྱས་འཕགས་པ་དེ་དགེ་འདུན་དཀོན་མཆོག་མ་ཡིན་པའི་ཕྱིར།](quote:p398-q04). "Otherwise it would follow that there are no final Three Jewels, because the Buddha-ārya would not be the Saṅgha Jewel." Haribhadra's exclusion is read there as restricting only the Saṅgha *addressed in this instruction*, not the Saṅgha Jewel as such. Put the two paragraphs together and the reasoning of p398 is complete. The Buddha-ārya is the Saṅgha Jewel. The Saṅgha as a whole culminates in that state. So the final refuge contains the Saṅgha. The phrase སྐྱབས་གསུམ་གྱི་ཟླས་ཕྱེ་བའི་ཚོགས is chosen with care. The ཚོགས of the verse is the Saṅgha as a *refuge*, counted as one of three, not any assembly whatever.

**What the paragraph leaves out: the Dharma.** The qualm concerns all three Jewels, but the answer argues only for the Saṅgha. The Dharma is left for the reader. Tsongkhapa does not leave it. In the Golden Rosary, at the corresponding place, he writes [མཐར་ཐུག་གི་སྐྱབས་ལ་དཀོན་མཆོག་གསུམ་མ་ཚང་བར་འགྱུར་ཞེ་ན། ཉེས་པ་མེད་དེ། སངས་རྒྱས་ཀྱི་ཐུགས་རྒྱུད་ཀྱི་འགོག་ལམ་ཆོས་ཡིན་ལ། ཆོས་ཀྱི་སྐུ་ཐོབ་པ་ན་ཐེག་པ་གསུམ་གྱི་ཚོགས་ཀྱང་མཐར་ཕྱིན་པར་འགྱུར་བའི་ཕྱིར་ཏེ།](quote:p398-q03). The cessation and path in the Buddha's mind are the Dharma, and when the dharmakāya is attained the assembly of the three vehicles too reaches its end. So this is the passage behind the yig cha's གསེར་ཕྲེང་ལས. Chökyi Gyaltsen has kept Tsongkhapa's second limb, attributed its purpose to him, and dropped the first. Kedrup Tenpa Dargye, in the Sera Mey overview, keeps all of it and adds the dharmakāya as a third limb: [མཐར་ཐུག་གི་སྐྱབས་ལ་དཀོན་མཆོག་གསུམ་མ་ཚང་བར་འགྱུར་རོ། །ཞེ་ན། སྐྱོན་མེད་དེ། ཐུབ་པ་སངས་རྒྱས་བཅོམ་ལྡན་འདས་ཆོས་ཀྱི་སྐུ་མཐར་ཐུག་པ་ཅན་ཡིན་པ་དང། དེའི་རྒྱུད་ཀྱི་འགོག་ལམ་གཉིས་ཆོས་དཀོན་མཆོག་ཡིན་པ་དང། སློབ་པ་འཕགས་པ་རྣམས་ཀྱང་ཆོས་སྐུ་དེའི་མཐར་ཐུག་པར་འགྲོ་བ་ཅན་ཡིན་པའི་ཕྱིར།](quote:p398-q11). The likeliest reason for Chökyi Gyaltsen's economy is that the Dharma limb needs no argument once p397 is in place. p397 has already made the Buddha "the one who has the Dharma that perfected the qualities of the two truths as his basis." The Dharma is therefore already built into the reason for the Buddha's being the final refuge, and only the Saṅgha needed saying. That is an inference from p397, not something the yig cha says.

**A shift in what the verse line is for.** In the Ratnagotravibhāga and its Vyākhyā, the fourth line is a *second reason why the Buddha alone* is the ultimate refuge. The assembly ends there, so the refuge it ends in is the one that is final. Asaṅga continues directly with "the ultimate refuge is one only" (དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས་ནི་གཅིག་ཉིད་དེ). Gyaltsab Je's word-commentary on the Uttaratantra reads it the same way: [ས་བཅུ་རྒྱུན་གྱི་ཐ་མ་མན་ཆད་ཐེག་པ་གསུམ་པོའི་འཕགས་པའི་ཚོགས་ཀྱང་ཆོས་སྐུ་དེའི་མཐར་ཐུག་པ་ཅན་དུ་འགྲོ་བ་ཡིན་པའི་ཕྱིར།](quote:p398-q10). Up to the last moment of the tenth ground, the ārya assembly of the three vehicles moves toward the dharmakāya as its culmination. That is why the Buddha is cause-refuge and result-refuge for them. Tsongkhapa, followed by this yig cha, turns the same line around. It stops being a reason for the Buddha's uniqueness and becomes the answer to an objection *raised by* that uniqueness. The two readings do not conflict: one line can both explain why the Buddha alone is final and show that nothing is lost by saying so. But the second use is Tibetan, and the yig cha's "stated in order to cut off the wrong idea" puts a purpose on the verse that its Indian commentator does not name.

**The definitions and their qualifier.** The phrase at the heart of both definitions, whether the traversal of the path has reached its end *as the thing actually stands* (དངོས་པོའི་གནས་ཚོད་ལ), comes from Gyaltsab Je's རྣམ་བཤད་སྙིང་པོའི་རྒྱན།: [དེ་ལ་དོན་དམ་པ་དང་། ཀུན་རྫོབ་པའི་སྐྱབས་ཀྱི་དོན་ནི། ཉན་ཐོས་རིགས་ངེས་པ་ལྟར། རང་གི་བསམ་ངོར་འཁོར་བའི་སྡུག་བསྔལ་ཞི་བ་ཙམ་ཞིག་དོན་དུ་གཉེར་ཞིང་དེ་ལས་གོང་དུ་བགྲོད་མི་དགོས་པར་འཛིན་ཅིང་། དངོས་པོའི་གནས་ཚོད་ལ་ལམ་གྱི་བགྲོད་པ་མཐར་མ་ཐུག་པའི་སྐྱབས་དེ་ཀུན་རྫོབ་པའི་སྐྱབས་དང་། བསམ་ངོར་ཡང་བགྲོད་པ་མཐར་ཐུག་པར་འཛིན་ཅིང་། དོན་ལ་ཡང་མཐར་ཐུག་པ་དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས་ཀྱི་དོན་ཡིན་གྱི།](quote:p398-q09). Gyaltsab's account has two sides. The first is how the seeker holds it: a śrāvaka of fixed lineage takes the pacification of saṃsāra's suffering as enough (བསམ་ངོར). The second is how it really is (དངོས་པོའི་གནས་ཚོད་ལ, དོན་ལ). Chökyi Gyaltsen keeps only the second. His definitions contain no reference to what anyone takes the refuge to be. That makes them cleaner as definitions, and it matters in p399, where the question is whether things like scripture are final refuges in fact, not whether someone regards them as final.

The first clause of the conventional definition needs explaining. It excludes anything such that "an ultimate refuge either is it or is a component of it." The reason is given in Chökyi Gyaltsen's own མཐའ་དཔྱོད on this chapter, which raises the objection it answers: [རྩོད་པ་སྤོང་བ་ལ་ཁོ་ན་རེ། སངས་རྒྱས་འཕགས་པ་དང་། བྱང་སེམས་མཐོང་ལམ་པ་གཉིས་ཀ་ཆོས་ཅན། ཀུན་རྫོབ་པའི་སྐྱབས་ཡིན་པར་ཐལ། དངོས་པོའི་གནས་ཚོད་ལ་ལམ་གྱི་བགྲོད་པ་མཐར་མ་ཐུག་པའི་སྐྱབས་ཡིན་པའི་ཕྱིར།](quote:p398-q07). Take the Buddha-ārya and the path-of-seeing bodhisattva *together* as the subject. That pair is a refuge, and it is not one whose path has reached its end. So the bare definition makes it a conventional refuge, with a Buddha inside it. His first answer there is an analogy: the non-returner, who need not fall under either member of a division taken collectively. Then he offers an alternative wording: [ཡང་ན་སྔར་གྱི་མཚན་ཉིད་ཀྱི་ཟུར་དུ་ཁྱོད་ཡིན་པའམ། ཁྱོད་ཀྱི་ནང་ཚན་དུ་གྱུར་པའི་དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས་མེད་པ་གང་ཞིག དངོས་པོའི་གནས་ཚོད་ལ་ལམ་གྱི་བགྲོད་པ་མཐར་མ་ཐུག་པའི་སྐྱབས་དེ། ཀུན་རྫོབ་པའི་སྐྱབས་ཀྱི་མཚན་ཉིད། ཁྱོད་ཡིན་པ་འམ། ཁྱོད་ཀྱི་ནང་ཚན་དུ་གྱུར་པའི་གནས་སྐབས་ཀྱི་སྐྱབས་མེད་པ་གང་ཞིག དངོས་པོའི་གནས་ཚོད་ལ་ལམ་གྱི་བགྲོད་པ་མཐར་ཐུག་པའི་སྐྱབས་དེ། གཅིག་ཤོས་ཀྱི་མཚན་ཉིད། ཅེས་བརྗོད་དོ། །](quote:p398-q08). This paragraph adopts the qualified conventional definition, but not the matching qualifier on the ultimate one. The mtha' dpyod qualifies both definitions symmetrically; the spyi don qualifies only one. The likeliest reason is that the ultimate definition does not need the qualifier: a composite containing an unfinished member has not "reached the end of the path" in fact, so it already fails the bare ultimate definition. I have not found Chökyi Gyaltsen saying so, and the asymmetry should be noted rather than explained away.

**Sera Jey specificity.** Comparing the two Sera yig chas at this exact point is instructive. They answer the same qualm with the same verse. Kedrup Tenpa Dargye (Sera Mey) gives the full three-limbed answer, dharmakāya, Dharma and Saṅgha. Chökyi Gyaltsen (Sera Jey) gives the Saṅgha limb alone. He relies on p397 for the Dharma and on p386 for the Buddha's membership in the Saṅgha, and he adds the formal definitions. In the lines I read around it, the Sera Mey passage at this point turns instead to whether every conventional body is a conventional refuge. The Sera Jey text is characteristically spare in its reasons and exact in its definitions. The formal definitions are also, as far as the sources checked here show, Chökyi Gyaltsen's own crystallisation of Gyaltsab Je's gloss.

## 5 · Bones of Contention 🦴

**What is disputed?** Three things lie close under this paragraph. The first is what, within Buddhahood, counts as the final Saṅgha and the final Dharma. The second is whether the form bodies are the final refuge. The third is how the line between conventional and ultimate refuge should be drawn.

**Who argues what?**

- **Which part of the Buddha is the final Saṅgha.** Chökyi Gyaltsen does not say. He says only that the Saṅgha "culminates" at Buddhahood, and p386 makes the Buddha-ārya, the *person*, the Saṅgha Jewel. Tsongkhapa and Kedrup Tenpa Dargye also speak of the assembly culminating, without naming a part. One text in this archive does name it: [མཐར་ཐུག་གི་སྐབས་ལ་ཆོས་དང་དགེ་འདུན་དཀོན་མཆོག་གཉིས་ཀྱང་ཚང་སྟེ། ཐུབ་པ་སངས་རྒྱས་བཅོམ་ལྡན་འདས་ལྟ་བུའི་ཐུགས་རྒྱུད་ཀྱི་སྤངས་རྟོགས་ཀྱི་ཆོས་རྣམས་མཐར་ཐུག་གི་ཆོས་དང་། གཟུགས་སྐུ་རྣམ་པ་གཉིས་པོ་ལྟ་བུ་དེ་མཐར་ཐུག་གི་དགེ་འདུན་དཀོན་མཆོག་ཡིན་པའི་ཕྱིར།](quote:p398-q12). There, the abandonments and realizations in the Buddha's mind are the final Dharma, and the two form bodies are the final Saṅgha Jewel. This is Jamyang Shantipa Lodrö Gyaltsen's སྐབས་བརྒྱད་ཀྱི་སྤྱི་དོན་མེ་ལོང་། (tier 5, the text furthest from this curriculum). It is a stronger and more specific claim than anything in the yig cha.
- **Whether the form bodies are a final refuge.** The ལུང་རིགས་ཀྱི་རོལ་མཚོ, catalogued here under Ngok Loden Sherab (tier 5; I have not checked the attribution), says the final refuge is the dharmakāya alone. It dismisses the alternatives outright: [གང་དག དཀོན་མཆོག་ཕྱི་མ་གཉིས་ཀྱི་མཚན་ཉིད་གང་ཡིན་ཐམས་ཅད་མཐར་ཐུག་གི་སྐྱབས་མ་ཡིན་པ་དང་། གཟུགས་སྐུ་གཉིས་ཀྱང་མཐར་ཐུག་གི་སྐྱབས་སུ་འཆད་པའི་ལུགས་སུ་བྱས་ནས་བཤད་པ་བྱེད་པ་དེ་ཐམས་ཅད་ནི་རྣམ་པར་འཁྱམས་སོ། །](quote:p398-q13). It goes on to argue that the two form bodies are *not* final refuges, because they are conventional truths. Gyaltsab Je holds the opposite in the རྣམ་བཤད་སྙིང་པོའི་རྒྱན།: [སྐུ་གསུམ་གྱི་བདག་ཉིད་ཅན་གྱི་སངས་རྒྱས་ཁོ་ན་དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས་སོ། །](quote:p398-q14). The ultimate refuge is the Buddha *who has the nature of the three bodies*. Chökyi Gyaltsen sides with Gyaltsab in p399. There he turns the Rong ṭīk's "compounded, therefore deceptive, therefore not final" against the form bodies, as a consequence the Rong ṭīk cannot accept. That only works if the form bodies *are* final refuges for him. This is a real conflict. The Rol mtsho position directly contradicts the Gelug position, and the yig cha follows the tier-2 text.
- **How to draw the line.** Chökyi Gyaltsen uses one criterion: has the path been traversed to its end, as things actually stand? Gyaltsab's gloss has two sides, as above: how the refuge is held (བསམ་ངོར) and how it really is. Jamyang Zhepa, for Gomang, keeps the two-sided form and phrases it through appearance: [ལམ་མམ་དགོས་དོན་མཐར་ཐུག་པར་རྫོགས་པར་སྣང་ཞིང་། དོན་ལའང་དགོས་དོན་མཐར་ཐུག་པར་རྫོགས་པའི་ཆ་ནས་བཞག་པའི་སྐྱབས་དེ། དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས་ཀྱི་མཚན་ཉིད།](quote:p398-q15). The ultimate refuge is one that *appears* complete and *is* complete. He defines the conventional refuge correspondingly as one that appears complete without being so. He confines it to the stage of learner āryas, and he agrees with this paragraph that "conventional" and "provisional" (གནས་སྐབས) are equivalent. The two definitions will usually pick out the same things. But Jamyang Zhepa's makes appearance a component of the definition, and Chökyi Gyaltsen's does not.
- **The Rong ṭīk (p399).** The Rong ṭīk denies final-refuge status to scripture, to the learner's path and cessation, and to learner āryas. This paragraph's definitions are the measure Chökyi Gyaltsen will use to decide which of those denials stand and which of the Rong ṭīk's *reasons* overreach. The disagreement in p399 is less about the verdicts than about the grounds.

**A note on the weight of this evidence.** The positions that disagree with the yig cha, the Rol mtsho and the Me long, are both tier 5. The support for the yig cha, Tsongkhapa and Gyaltsab, is tier 2, and the Sera Mey parallel is from the Sungbum. Nothing was found at tier 3. The attribution to the Golden Rosary could only be matched in substance, not in words. See §2.

**Why does it matter?** Going for refuge in this tradition is going for refuge to three things. If the final object of refuge is one Buddha, it has to be shown that nothing of the three is lost at the final level. Otherwise the Dharma and the Saṅgha would be merely instrumental, abandoned like the raft, and the Saṅgha Jewel would be a provisional convenience. The answer protects the threefold refuge *as final*. It does so at a precise cost. The Buddha must count as a member of the Saṅgha, which is why p386 fights so hard over Haribhadra's "arhat included within the Buddha Jewel."

**Resolution.** The Sera Jey position is as follows. The Buddha alone is the final refuge, and the Three Jewels are complete in him, because the Saṅgha culminates in Buddhahood and the Buddha-ārya is himself the Saṅgha Jewel. The conventional and ultimate refuges are distinguished by one factual criterion, whether the traversal of the path has reached its end. The conventional definition carries a qualifier excluding any composite that has an ultimate refuge as a component. The Buddha "of three bodies" is the final refuge, form bodies included.

## 6 · Key Terminology & Technical Concepts

- **མཐར་ཐུག་གི་སྐྱབས། / དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས།** — final refuge / ultimate refuge; declared equivalent here.
  - **Source of the term:** the Ratnagotravibhāga's དམ་པའི་དོན་དུ … སྐྱབས and its ཚོགས་ཀྱང་དེ་ཡི་མཐར་ཐུག.
  - **How understood here:** the equivalence is doing work. "Ultimate" here does not mean "ultimate truth." The form bodies are conventional truths and are still final refuges for Gyaltsab and for Chökyi Gyaltsen. It means "final": a refuge beyond which there is no further stage to go.
  - **Debates:** the Rol mtsho reads "ultimate" much closer to "ultimate truth," which is why it excludes the form bodies. See §5.

- **ཀུན་རྫོབ་པའི་སྐྱབས། / གནས་སྐབས་ཀྱི་སྐྱབས།** — conventional refuge / provisional refuge; also declared equivalent.
  - **How understood here:** a refuge on the way, not a false one. The learner's Dharma and Saṅgha really protect. They are simply not where the path ends.
  - **Debates:** Jamyang Zhepa likewise equates it with the provisional refuge and with the "refuge that still has fear" (འཇིགས་བཅས་ཀྱི་སྐྱབས). He confines it to the stage of learner āryas.

- **དངོས་པོའི་གནས་ཚོད་ལ་ལམ་གྱི་བགྲོད་པ་མཐར་ཐུག་པ།** — "the traversal of the path having reached its end, as the thing actually stands."
  - **How understood here:** the one criterion of both definitions. The qualification "as it actually stands" sets aside how a practitioner regards the refuge. In Gyaltsab Je's gloss, that subjective side (བསམ་ངོར) was the other half of the account.
  - **Debates:** Gyaltsab and Jamyang Zhepa keep both sides; Chökyi Gyaltsen keeps one.

- **ཁྱོད་ཡིན་པའམ་ཁྱོད་ཀྱི་ནང་ཚན་དུ་གྱུར་པའི་དོན་དམ་པའི་སྐྱབས་མེད་པ།** — "such that there is no ultimate refuge which is it or is a component of it."
  - **How understood here:** a guard against composites. Without it, a Buddha taken together with a bodhisattva would pass as a conventional refuge, as the objection in the mtha' dpyod shows.
  - **Debates:** the mtha' dpyod offers it only as an alternative ("ཡང་ན") to answering by analogy with the non-returner, and it adds a matching qualifier to the ultimate definition. The spyi don adopts the qualifier for the conventional definition only.

- **སྐྱབས་གསུམ་གྱི་ཟླས་ཕྱེ་བའི་ཚོགས།** — "the assembly as marked off by its pairing with the three refuges."
  - **How understood here:** it fixes the reference of ཚོགས in the Uttaratantra line. The assembly meant there is the Saṅgha *qua* one of the three refuges, not assemblies in general and not the ārya assembly of the preceding verse considered simply as learners. That is what lets its culmination count as the Saṅgha's presence within the final refuge.
