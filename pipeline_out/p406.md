# The flesh eye — a hundred leagues to a trichiliocosm, and what "each determinate" means

## 1 · Passage & Context

**Source Text:**

> གཉིས་པ་ལ་ལྔ་ལས། དང་པོ་ནི། ཤའི་སྤྱན་ལ་གདམས་པ་ནི། མདོ་ལས༑ བཅོམ་ལྡན་འདས་ཀྱིས་བཀའ་སྩལ་པ། ཤཱ་རི་དྭ་ཏིའི་བུ་བྱང་ཆེན་ཤའི་མིག་གིས་དཔག་ཚད་བརྒྱ་མཐོང་བ་ཡོད་དོ། ། ཞེས་པ་ནས། སྟོང་གསུམ་གྱི་འཇིག་རྟེན་གྱི་ཁམས་མཐོང་བ་ཡོད་དོ། ། ཞེས་པའི་བར་གྱིས་བསྟན། དེའི་དོན་འཆད་པ་ལ། གྲངས་ཇི་ལྟ་བ་བཞིན་དུ་དངོས་པོ་སོ་སོར་ངེས་པ་དང་། ཞེས་གསུངས།

**Preceding Section:** p405 opens the treatise-side exposition of the sixth of the ten instructions. It says the root text's སྤྱན་ལྔ་དང་ནི། is explained word by word by Haribhadra's ཤའི་མིག་དང་། and so on, and divides that explanation into the actual word-meaning and a summary. The actual word-meaning has three parts: instruction by way of the *names* of the eyes, instruction by way of their *meaning*, and the manner in which the instruction is given. p405 then disposes of the first: the sūtra's list ཤའི་མིག་དང་། ལྷའི་མིག་དང་། … སངས་རྒྱས་ཀྱི་མིག་གོ། is what Haribhadra's list of the five eyes (ཤའི་མིག་དང་རྣམ་པར་སྨིན་པ་ལས་བྱུང་བའི་ལྷའི་དང་ཤེས་རབ་དང་ཆོས་དང་སངས་རྒྱས་ཀྱི་སྤྱན།) explains.

**Following Section:** p407 takes up the second of the five, the divine eye. It quotes the sūtra's answer to Śāriputra, that a bodhisattva fully knows the divine eye of the gods of the Four Great Kings' realm and above, and matches it to Haribhadra's next phrase, སེམས་ཅན་ཐམས་ཅད་ཀྱི་འཆི་འཕོ་བ་དང་། སྐྱེ་བ་དང་།. The wisdom, dharma and buddha eyes follow in the same format. After them the text states the "manner of instruction", and only then does it start the debate (མཐའ་དཔྱད་པ) with definitions.

**What this paragraph is doing.** It opens the second part, instruction by way of the meaning, which has five sub-parts, one per eye. Each sub-part has the same form. The yig cha marks off a span of the Twenty-Five Thousand by its first and last words (ཞེས་པ་ནས … ཞེས་པའི་བར), then names the phrase of Haribhadra that explains it. For the flesh eye that span runs from a hundred leagues to a trichiliocosm, and the phrase is གྲངས་ཇི་ལྟ་བ་བཞིན་དུ་དངོས་པོ་སོ་སོར་ངེས་པ་དང་།. The paragraph argues nothing. Its work is correlation: it fixes which words of the commentary gloss which words of the sūtra, so that the debate a few pages on has an agreed text to argue over.

**On the elision.** The sūtra quotation is shortened twice. First, བྱང་ཆེན stands for the sūtra's བྱང་ཆུབ་སེམས་དཔའ་སེམས་དཔའ་ཆེན་པོ, as throughout this text. Second, the ཞེས་པ་ནས … ཞེས་པའི་བར frame leaves out every rung of the list between the first and last. Those rungs matter for the debate later, and they are in the Twenty-Five Thousand in the Kangyur. After the first rung, [ཤཱ་རདྭ་ཏིའི་བུ། བྱང་ཆུབ་སེམས་དཔའ་སེམས་དཔའ་ཆེན་པོ་ཤའི་མིག་གིས་དཔག་ཚད་བརྒྱ་མཐོང་བ་ཡོད་དོ། …](quote:p406-q01), come two, three, four and five hundred leagues and a thousand, then Jambudvīpa, two, three and four continents, a thousand-world system and a two-thousand-world system. The last rung is followed by the sūtra's own summary: [ཤཱ་རདྭ་ཏིའི་བུ། འདི་ནི་བྱང་ཆུབ་སེམས་དཔའ་སེམས་དཔའ་ཆེན་པོའི་ཤའི་མིག་ཡོངས་སུ་དག་པའོ།](quote:p406-q02).

**On ཡོད་དོ.** The Tibetan says only "there is [one that] sees" (མཐོང་བ་ཡོད་དོ), and I have translated it that way on purpose. The sūtra does not say that *the* bodhisattva's flesh eye sees a hundred leagues. It says there exists a flesh eye that does, and another that sees two hundred, and so on. As §5 shows, the debate turns on this existential form.

**On the gloss.** གྲངས་ཇི་ལྟ་བ་བཞིན་དུ is not part of the definition of the flesh eye. It is Haribhadra's device for pairing his list of five eyes with his list of five objects in order. The yig cha quotes it with the first object because the first object is where it stands.

## 3 · Structural Context (Dkar Chag)

- **Abhisamayālaṃkāra location:** chapter one, the ten instructions (གདམས་ངག་བཅུ) under the second topic, sarvākārajñatā. The pack's outline path names no lemma, but p405, one paragraph earlier, quotes the root words it is explaining (སྤྱན་ལྔ་དང་ནི།). They are in the verse [མ་ཞེན་ཡོངས་སུ་མི་ངལ་དང་། ། ལམ་ནི་ཡོངས་སུ་འཛིན་པ་དང་། ། སྤྱན་ལྔ་དང་ནི་མངོན་ཤེས་ཀྱི། ། ཡོན་ཏན་དྲུག་དང་མཐོང་ལམ་དང་། ། བསྒོམ་ཞེས་བྱ་ལ་གདམས་ངག་ནི། ། བཅུ་ཡི་བདག་ཉིད་ཤེས་པར་བྱ། །](quote:p406-q03). The five eyes are the sixth instruction. The *Ornament* gives them four syllables and says nothing about any single eye. The lemma for this paragraph is therefore that phrase, and I infer it from p405 rather than taking it from the pack.
- **Haribhadra's commentary location:** the paragraph quotes Haribhadra's gloss, and I read it in place in the འགྲེལ་པ་དོན་གསལ། at the aligned passage: [ཤའི་དང་། རྣམ་པར་སྨིན་པ་ལས་བྱུང་བའི་ལྷའི་དང་། ཤེས་རབ་དང་ཆོས་དང་སངས་རྒྱས་ཀྱི་སྤྱན་གྲངས་ཇི་ལྟ་བ་བཞིན་དུ་དངོས་པོ་སོ་སོར་ངེས་པ་དང་། སེམས་ཅན་ཐམས་ཅད་ཀྱི་འཆི་འཕོ་བ་དང་། …](quote:p406-q04). The five eyes and their five objects make up a single sentence, and the sentence closes by saying the student is taught to realise all these subjects as one in suchness. Several things Haribhadra does *not* say are worth recording. He gives no range for the flesh eye: nothing about a hundred leagues and nothing about a trichiliocosm. He gives no reason why its objects are "determinate". He does not say whether the eye is a sense faculty or a knower. He puts "born of ripening" (རྣམ་པར་སྨིན་པ་ལས་བྱུང་བ) on the *divine* eye, not the flesh eye. Everything the Tibetan debate later argues about comes from the sūtra and from Ārya Vimuktisena, not from Haribhadra.
- **Text location:** under སྤྱན་ལྔ་ལ་གདམས་པ (from p404), the spyi don has three parts: the sūtra, how the treatise explains it, and the debate (མདོ་ལས་གསུངས་ཚུལ། བསྟན་བཅོས་ཀྱིས་འགྲེལ་ཚུལ། མཐའ་དཔྱད་པ). This paragraph is in the second. Inside that, it falls under དང་པོ་ཚིག་དོན་དངོས (p405), then ༢༽ དོན་གྱི་སྒོ་ནས་གདམས་པ, whose five sub-parts are the five eyes. This is the first of them. I checked the path in the text itself, and it matches the pack.
- **Section type:** preliminary exposition of text (ཚིག་དོན). It correlates sūtra with commentary and has no ཁ་ཅིག and no ཐལ་འགྱུར.
- **Argumentative role:** it sets up the proof-text. The debate on the flesh eye's definition a few pages on is decided by the first rung of this sūtra list, the rung this paragraph quotes in full. What looks like bookkeeping here is laying down the premise that settles the controversy later.

## 4 · Philosophical Exposition & Commentary

**Core meaning.** The paragraph asserts that the sūtra's graded list, from a hundred leagues to a trichiliocosm, is what Haribhadra means by the flesh eye's object, "things each determinate". The rest of this section asks two questions: determinate in what sense, and why the list stops where it does.

**Ārya Vimuktisena supplies what Haribhadra leaves out.** The Indian source for Haribhadra's compressed phrase is the ཉི་ཁྲི་སྣང་བ།. It marks off exactly the span of sūtra this paragraph marks off, and then explains it: [ཤའི་མིག་གིས་དཔག་ཚད་བརྒྱ་མཐོང་བ་ཡོད་དོ་ཞེས་བྱ་བ་ནས་བརྩམས་ཏེ། … དེ་ནི་རྣམ་པར་སྨིན་པ་ལས་བྱུང་བ་ཉིད་ཀྱིས་ཡུལ་སོ་སོར་ངེས་པ་ཉིད་ཡིན་པའི་ཕྱིར་རམ། ཤའི་མིག་ནི་བདག་པོའི་འབྲས་བུའི་ཡུལ་ཅན་ཡིན་ལ། དེ་ཡང་སྟོང་གསུམ་གྱི་སྟོང་ཆེན་པོའི་འཇིག་རྟེན་གྱི་ཁམས་ཀྱིས་བསྡུས་པ་ཡིན་པའི་ཕྱིར་རོ།](quote:p406-q05). He says the objects are "each determinate" because the eye is born of ripening. Alternatively, it is because the flesh eye takes as its object a dominant result (བདག་པོའི་འབྲས་བུ), and that result is contained within a trichiliocosm. Haribhadra's ཡུལ་སོ་སོར་ངེས་པ is Vimuktisena's wording with the reasons removed. Vimuktisena is tier 4, an Indian commentary on the Twenty-Five Thousand at some distance from this yig cha. Every Gelug author below who explains "determinate" does so through him, usually under the short title ཉི་སྣང.

**Tsongkhapa: how to parse Haribhadra, and why the limit.** In the ལེགས་བཤད་གསེར་ཕྲེང Tsongkhapa settles the grammar first: [སྔ་མ་ལྔ་མིང་དང་ཕྱི་མ་ལྔ་དོན་ཡིན་པས་མིང་དོན་གྲངས་བཞིན་དུ་སྦྱར་རོ། ། དེ་ལ་ཤའི་སྤྱན་ནི། དཔག་ཚད་བརྒྱ་ནས་སྟོང་གསུམ་ཚུན་ཆད་ཀྱི་དངོས་པོ་རྣམས་སོ་སོར་ངེས་པའི་ཡུལ་ཅན་ཏེ།](quote:p406-q06). The first five items are names and the second five are meanings, matched in order. That is the parse this paragraph relies on when it takes one name and one meaning out of a ten-item sentence. Tsongkhapa's paraphrase also brings the sūtra's range into Haribhadra's phrase: "things from a hundred leagues up to a trichiliocosm, each determinate". He then reads Vimuktisena as giving two separate reasons for the restriction, [ལུང་དེས་ཡུལ་ངེས་པའི་རྒྱུ་མཚན་གཉིས་བསྟན་ཏེ། དང་པོ་ནི་ལས་ཀྱི་རྣམ་སྨིན་ལས་བྱུང་བའི་ཕྱིར་ཞེས་པ་ཡིན་ལ། གཉིས་པ་ནི། ཤའི་མིག་ནི་བདག་འབྲས་ཀྱི་ཡུལ་ཅན་ཡིན་པའི་ཕྱིར་ཞེས་པའོ།](quote:p406-q07), and explains why the second reason holds: [དབང་པོས་ཀྱང་རང་གི་བདག་འབྲས་མ་ཡིན་པ་ལ་ལོངས་སྤྱོད་མི་ནུས་པས་ཡུལ་ངེས་ཏེ། སེམས་ཅན་རྣམས་ཀྱི་ཐུན་མོང་གི་ལོངས་སྤྱོད་བྱ་ལ་སྟོང་གསུམ་གཅིག་ལས་མི་འགྲུབ་པས་སོ།](quote:p406-q08). A sense power can only enjoy what is its own dominant result. The shared environment that beings' collective karma produces never extends past one trichiliocosm. So the flesh eye, however refined, is an eye of this world-system. On Tsongkhapa's reading, "determinate" means *bounded*: the eye has a fixed ceiling, and that ceiling is the trichiliocosm.

**Gyaltsab Je and Panchen Sonam Drakpa** do the same thing in their word-glosses (the pack's passages from the རྣམ་བཤད་སྙིང་པོའི་རྒྱན། and the འགྲེལ་ཚིག་དོན་རབ་གསལ།). Each folds "a hundred leagues up to a trichiliocosm" straight into Haribhadra's phrase, so that "things each determinate" becomes "coarse and subtle forms from a hundred leagues up to a trichiliocosm". Neither raises Vimuktisena's reasons at that point. They are glossing, and the question of the limit is left to the debate texts.

**Chökyi Gyaltsen's own view.** Here the yig cha only correlates, but its author's position is fixed shortly afterwards in the same work, where the debate section gives the definition: [སྤྱན་ལྔའི་ནང་ཚན་གང་ཞིག རང་གི་ཐུན་མོང་མ་ཡིན་པའི་བདག་རྐྱེན་དུ་གྱུར་པའི་ཤའི་མིག་ལ་བརྟེན་ནས་དཔག་ཚད་བརྒྱའི་གཟུགས་ཕྲ་རགས་མངོན་སུམ་དུ་མཐོང་བའི་རིག་པ། ཤའི་སྤྱན་གྱི་མཚན་ཉིད།](quote:p406-q10). The defining range is a hundred leagues, the first rung of the list and the one this paragraph quotes in full. It is not the trichiliocosm at the top. The definition is built so that the least of the flesh eyes the sūtra names still counts. The same passage also presents the flesh eye as a *knower* (རིག་པ) that depends on the physical flesh eye as its uncommon dominant condition. It is not the physical organ itself. Straight after the definition, Chökyi Gyaltsen answers someone who concludes that the flesh eye would then have to be a consciousness: the fleshly organ has no object, so the pervasion fails.

**Sera Jey specificity.** On this point the curriculum is less distinctive than it might seem: as §5 shows, Gomang reaches the same result from the same sūtra line. What is characteristic of the yig cha is where it puts the weight. The flesh eye's range is fixed from its *lower* edge, the first rung, which this paragraph quotes in full while eliding the rest. The upper limit, which is what the Indian commentaries and the gser phreng explain, is left alone.

## 5 · Bones of Contention 🦴

This paragraph is not itself a debate. It opens no ཁ་ཅིག and draws no consequence. But the sentence it quotes is the proof-text for a real dispute about the flesh eye, and that dispute is the reason the yig cha quotes the first rung verbatim and elides the rest.

**What is disputed?** Whether the sūtra's list, from a hundred leagues to a trichiliocosm, gives the reach of *every* flesh eye or a series of grades held by different bodhisattvas. On the first reading, a flesh eye as such sees everything up to a trichiliocosm. On the second, the defining minimum is a hundred leagues, and the trichiliocosm is only the highest grade.

**Who argues what?**

- **The ཁ་ཅིག in Chökyi Gyaltsen's debate manual** takes the first reading. In the སྐབས་དང་པོའི་མཐའ་དཔྱོད།, under the heading ཤའི་དང་རྣམ་པར་སྨིན་པ་ལས་བྱུང་བའི། ཞེས་སོགས་ཀྱི་སྐབས་སུ།, the opponent defines the flesh eye as [ཁ་ཅིག སྤྱན་ལྔའི་ནང་ཚན་ཡིན་པ་གང་ཞིག རང་གི་བདག་རྐྱེན་དུ་གྱུར་པའི་ཤའི་མིག་ལས་བྱུང་ཞིང་སྟོང་གསུམ་ཚུན་ཆད་ཀྱི་གཟུགས་ཕྲ་རགས་མངོན་སུམ་དུ་མཐོང་བ་དེ། ཤའི་སྤྱན་གྱི་མཚན་ཉིད།](quote:p406-q11). The position is natural if the Tsongkhapa–Gyaltsab gloss ("from a hundred leagues up to a trichiliocosm") is taken as describing one eye's reach.
- **Jetsun Chökyi Gyaltsen** refutes it with the sentence this paragraph quotes: [མདོ་ལས། ཤཱ་རིའི་བུ། བྱང་ཆུབ་སེམས་དཔའ་སེམས་དཔའ་ཆེན་པོ་ཤའི་མིག་གིས་དཔག་ཚད་བརྒྱ་མཐོང་བ་ཡོད་དེ་ཞེས་གསུངས་པ་མི་འཐད་པར་ཐལ། དཔག་ཚད་བརྒྱའི་གཟུགས་ཕྲ་རགས་མངོན་སུམ་དུ་མཐོང་ལ། དཔག་ཚད་ཉིས་བརྒྱའི་གཟུགས་ཕྲ་རགས་མངོན་སུམ་དུ་མ་མཐོང་བའི་ཤའི་སྤྱན་མེད་པའི་ཕྱིར།](quote:p406-q12). If every flesh eye saw a trichiliocosm, there would be no flesh eye that sees a hundred leagues but not two hundred, and the sūtra's "there is [one] that sees a hundred leagues" would be false. The argument depends on reading ཡོད་དོ as asserting existence, which is why §2 translates it that way.
- **An Indian witness to the graded reading.** Daṃṣṭrasena's commentary on the Hundred Thousand (tier 4) reads the same list in the same way, as a ranking across bodhisattvas: [བྱང་ཆུབ་སེམས་དཔའི་ས་མཐོན་དམན་དང་། ཡོན་ཏན་ཆེ་ཆུང་གི་རིམ་པ་ལས། ལ་ལའི་ཤའི་མིག་གིས་ནི་དཔག་ཚད་བརྒྱ་ཚུན་ཆད་མཐོང་། ལ་ལས་ནི་དཔག་ཚད་སྟོང་མཐོང་། ལ་ལས་ནི་སྟོང་གསུམ་གྱི་སྟོང་ཆེན་པོའི་འཇིག་རྟེན་གྱི་ཁམས་ཀུན་མཐོང་བ་ཡང་ཡོད་དེ།](quote:p406-q13). So Chökyi Gyaltsen's reading is not a Tibetan innovation. It is the plain sense of the sūtra, as an Indian commentator already read it.
- **Gomang agrees.** Jamyang Zhepa's ཕར་ཕྱིན་མཐའ་དཔྱོད་རིན་ཆེན་སྒྲོན་མེ། (tier 5) refutes a definition of the same shape by the same route and counts the rungs: [ཤའི་མིག་ལ་དཔག་ཚད་བརྒྱ་མཐོང་བ་ཡང་ཡོད། … སྟོང་གསུམ་མཐོང་བའི་བྱང་སེམས་ཀྱི་ཤའི་མི་ག་གི་བར་མཐོ་དམན་བཅུ་གསུམ་ཡོད་པའི་ཕྱིར།](quote:p406-q14). There are thirteen grades, from the beginner who sees only a hundred leagues up to the one who sees a trichiliocosm. In the same passage he adds a fourteenth, the Buddha's flesh eye, which is not limited to a trichiliocosm. That opens a separate question: how Vimuktisena's ceiling can apply to a Buddha. I have not traced how the Sera Jey texts handle it.

**A second, quieter divergence: what the two reasons establish.** Tsongkhapa takes both of Vimuktisena's reasons as fixing the *upper* limit (quote q07 above). Jamyang Śāntipa's སྐབས་བརྒྱད་ཀྱི་སྤྱི་དོན་མེ་ལོང་། (tier 5) divides them differently: [འདི་དག་གིས་ཡུལ་སོ་སོར་ངེས་པའི་རྒྱུ་མཚན་གཉིས་སྟོན་པ་ཡིན་ནོ། ། སྔ་མས་དཔག་ཚད་བརྒྱ་ཚུན་ཆད་མཐོང་བར་བསྟན་ལ། ཕྱི་མས་སྟོང་གསུམ་ཕན་ཆད་མི་མཐོང་བར་བསྟན་པས།](quote:p406-q09). On his reading, "born of ripening" accounts for the lower grade and "dominant result" for the ceiling. The difference is small, but it bears directly on this paragraph's gloss. For Tsongkhapa, སོ་སོར་ངེས་པ says that the flesh eye's field is bounded. For Śāntipa, it also says that the field is graded, a different reach for each eye according to its karmic cause. Chökyi Gyaltsen's definition, which is set at the lower edge, fits Śāntipa's division more naturally. However, I found no passage in which Chökyi Gyaltsen comments on Vimuktisena's two reasons, so I cannot say which division he would take.

**A textual oddity worth marking.** Vimuktisena makes "born of ripening" a property of the *flesh* eye. Haribhadra's sentence attaches it to the *divine* eye (རྣམ་པར་སྨིན་པ་ལས་བྱུང་བའི་ལྷའི་), and so does the yig cha's own list at p405. Tsongkhapa follows Vimuktisena for the flesh eye (ལས་ཀྱི་རྣམ་སྨིན་ལས་བྱུང་བ). Gyaltsab Je, glossing Haribhadra, gives ripening from contaminated virtue to the divine eye. The two ascriptions are compatible: nothing prevents both eyes from being ripened. But anyone deriving the flesh eye's limit "from ripening" is using Vimuktisena's words, not Haribhadra's. I report this and do not resolve it. None of the sources I read raises it.

**Why does it matter?** A definition set at the ceiling would exclude every bodhisattva below the top grade from having a flesh eye at all, and the sūtra explicitly says such eyes exist. A definition set at the floor keeps the sūtra true and turns the rest of the list into a description of growth. The flesh eye then becomes something that develops along the path, which fits Gyaltsab Je's placement of its first attainment on the Mahāyāna path of accumulation (ཤའི་སྤྱན་ཚོགས་ལམ་ནས་).

**Resolution.** Sera Jey follows Chökyi Gyaltsen and defines the flesh eye by the hundred-league minimum. The justification is the sūtra sentence quoted in full in this paragraph, read as asserting existence. Gomang reaches the same result by the same argument. The authors differ on how Vimuktisena's reasons divide the work, not on the grading itself.

## 6 · Key Terminology & Technical Concepts

- **ཤའི་སྤྱན།** — the flesh eye, first of the five eyes.
  - **Sanskrit equivalent:** *māṃsa-cakṣus*.
  - **How understood here:** Chökyi Gyaltsen distinguishes ཤའི་སྤྱན from ཤའི་མིག. The first is a *knower* (རིག་པ) that sees coarse and subtle forms directly. The second is the special physical organ it depends on as its uncommon dominant condition. The sūtra says ཤའི་མིག throughout, Haribhadra's gloss says སྤྱན, and the spyi don uses both words with care.
  - **Debates:** whether the flesh eye is a sense consciousness or a mental one. Chökyi Gyaltsen's debate manual refutes a definition that would make it a sense consciousness, on the ground that sense perception cannot see through obstructions. Jamyang Zhepa likewise presses the consequence that such definitions would make it a sense consciousness, and rejects it.

- **དངོས་པོ་སོ་སོར་ངེས་པ།** — "things each determinate": the flesh eye's object in Haribhadra's gloss.
  - **How understood here:** Haribhadra takes the phrase from Vimuktisena's ཡུལ་སོ་སོར་ངེས་པ, and the reason Vimuktisena gave for the determinacy is lost on the way. In the Gelug commentaries it carries both the sūtra's range (a hundred leagues to a trichiliocosm) and Vimuktisena's two explanations of that range.
  - **Debates:** whether "determinate" means only *bounded above* (Tsongkhapa) or also *graded by karmic cause* (Jamyang Śāntipa's division). See §5.

- **གྲངས་ཇི་ལྟ་བ་བཞིན་དུ།** — "respectively".
  - **Sanskrit equivalent:** *yathāsaṃkhyam*.
  - **How understood here:** a parsing instruction, not content. Tsongkhapa spells it out as མིང་དོན་གྲངས་བཞིན་དུ་སྦྱར: five names, five meanings, matched in order. Its presence explains why the yig cha can split one sentence of Haribhadra across five paragraphs, p406 to p410, and still claim to be quoting him.

- **བདག་པོའི་འབྲས་བུ།** — dominant result.
  - **Sanskrit equivalent:** *adhipati-phala*.
  - **How understood here:** Vimuktisena's second reason, as Tsongkhapa explains it. The outer environment is the dominant result of beings' collective karma, and a sense power can engage only what is its own dominant result. That is why the world-system that such karma produces, a single trichiliocosm, is the ceiling of the flesh eye's reach.

- **དཔག་ཚད།** — league.
  - **Sanskrit equivalent:** *yojana*.
  - **How understood here:** its only role is as the first rung of the sūtra's list. The debate hangs on the fact that the list starts at a hundred of them, not on how long one is.
