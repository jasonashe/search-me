# The divine eye — sūtra passage and Haribhadra's gloss

## 1 · Passage & Context

**Source Text:**

> གཉིས་པ་ལྷའི་སྤྱན་ནི། ཤཱ་རིའི་བུས་གསོལ་པ། བཅོམ་ལྡན་འདས་བྱང་ཆེན་ལྷའི་མིག་ཡོངས་སུ་དག་པ་གང་ལགས། བཅོམ་ལྡན་འདས་ཀྱིས་བཀའ་སྩལ་པ། ཤཱ་རིའི་བུ་རྒྱལ་པོ་ཆེན་པོ་བཞིའི་རིགས་ཀྱི་ལྷ་རྣམས་ཀྱི་ལྷའི་མིག་གང་ཡིན་པ་དེ་ནི་བྱང་ཆུབ་སེམས་དཔས་རབ་ཏུ་ཤེས་སོ། ། ཞེས་སོགས་ཀྱི་དོན་འཆད་པ་ལ། སེམས་ཅན་ཐམས་ཅད་ཀྱི་འཆི་འཕོ་བ་དང་། སྐྱེ་བ་དང་། ཞེས་གསུངས།

**Preceding Section:** p406 is the first of the five items under "instruction by way of meaning" (དོན་གྱི་སྒོ་ནས་གདམས་པ), the flesh eye (ཤའི་སྤྱན). It gives the sūtra's range for the flesh eye, "from 'the great bodhisattva sees a hundred yojanas with the flesh eye' … to 'sees the trichiliocosm'" (དཔག་ཚད་བརྒྱ … སྟོང་གསུམ་གྱི་འཇིག་རྟེན་གྱི་ཁམས་མཐོང་བ་ཡོད་དོ།), and then the phrase of Haribhadra that explains it: གྲངས་ཇི་ལྟ་བ་བཞིན་དུ་དངོས་པོ་སོ་སོར་ངེས་པ་དང་།, "[the object of the first eye is] the distinct, delimited things, taken in order of enumeration." Behind p406 is p405, which set up the division: the root-verse words སྤྱན་ལྔ་དང་ནི།, then Haribhadra's naming of the five eyes, including the key qualifier རྣམ་པར་སྨིན་པ་ལས་བྱུང་བའི་ལྷའི་[སྤྱན], "the divine eye that arises from maturation."

**Following Section:** p408 does the same thing for the third eye, the wisdom eye (ཤེས་རབ་ཀྱི་སྤྱན). It quotes the sūtra passage in which the wisdom eye finds no dharma to know, whether conditioned or unconditioned, virtuous or non-virtuous, contaminated or uncontaminated. Haribhadra's gloss follows: ཆོས་ཐམས་ཅད་ལ་རྣམ་པར་མི་རྟོག་པ་དང་།, "non-conceptuality toward all dharmas." After that come the Dharma eye (p409) and the Buddha eye (p410). The argument begins only after all five, with the definitions and boundaries debated in p410–p412.

**What this paragraph is doing:** It does not argue. It is the second link in a chain of five matched pairs. Each pair gives a sūtra passage from the five-eyes section of the Twenty-Five Thousand and then the words of Haribhadra's འགྲེལ་པ་དོན་གསལ། that gloss it. The work lies in the matching. From a long sūtra passage about the gods' divine eyes, the paragraph picks out which words of Haribhadra state its point, and they are "the deaths and births of all beings." The paragraph cites only the sūtra's opening sentence and marks the cut with ཞེས་སོགས. What the gloss summarises is the part it leaves out.

**On the sūtra wording.** The yig cha's quotation matches Haribhadra's eight-chapter arrangement of the sūtra, [ཤཱ་རིའི་བུས་གསོལ་པ། … ཤཱ་རིའི་བུ་རྒྱལ་པོ་ཆེན་པོ་བཞིའི་རིས་ཀྱི་ལྷ་རྣམས་ཀྱི་ལྷའི་མིག་གང་ཡིན་པ་དེ་ནི་བྱང་ཆུབ་སེམས་དཔས་རབ་ཏུ་ཤེས་སོ། །](quote:p407-q03), almost word for word. The Kangyur's Twenty-Five Thousand differs slightly in wording: it has ཤཱ་རདྭ་ཏིའི་བུ and a bare གསོལ་པ ([the canonical text](quote:p407-q01)). Two readings in the yig cha look like scribal variants rather than doctrine. One is རིགས, "class," where both sūtra witnesses read རིས, "abode/realm." The other is the abbreviation བྱང་ཆེན.

**On the ellipsis.** "The Four Great Kings" is only the first of a list of god-realms that runs up to Akaniṣṭha. The Kangyur text then gives the half of the passage that Haribhadra's gloss is actually about: [ལྷའི་མིག་དེ་ལྟ་བུ་དང་ལྡན་པའི་བྱང་ཆུབ་སེམས་དཔའ་སེམས་དཔའ་ཆེན་པོས་ནི་ཤར་ཕྱོགས་ཀྱི་འཇིག་རྟེན་གྱི་ཁམས་གངྒཱའི་ཀླུང་གི་བྱེ་མ་སྙེད་ཀྱི་སེམས་ཅན་རྣམས་ཀྱི་འཆི་འཕོ་དང་སྐྱེ་བ་རབ་ཏུ་ཤེས་སོ། །](quote:p407-q02). In translation: "the great bodhisattva endowed with such a divine eye fully knows the deaths and births of the beings in eastern worlds as many as the Ganges' sands." The same is then said for the other nine directions. A reader who has only the paragraph could think Haribhadra drew "deaths and births" out of the Four Great Kings clause. He did not. The phrase sums up the second half of the passage, which the ཞེས་སོགས་ leaves out.

**On the grammar of the gloss.** Haribhadra's string is not a sentence. It is two members of a long list of objects (ཡུལ) that ends ཡུལ་ཅན་རྣམས, and each object is paired with one eye by order of enumeration. So "the deaths and births of all sentient beings" gives *the object* of the second eye. It is not a definition of the eye. That distinction is the whole issue of §5.

## 3 · Structural Context (Dkar Chag)

- **Abhisamayālaṃkāra location:** chapter one, the ten instructions (གདམས་ངག) that belong to the second topic, the mind generation and instructions. The root line is [སྤྱན་ལྔ་དང་ནི་མངོན་ཤེས་ཀྱི། ། ཡོན་ཏན་དྲུག་དང་མཐོང་ལམ་དང་། །](quote:p407-q04): "the five eyes, the six qualities of clairvoyance, the path of seeing, and …". The pack gave no lemma for this paragraph. The placement here comes from the text's own words at p404–p405 (སྤྱན་ལྔ་དང་ནི། ཞེས་པས་ … སྤྱན་ལྔ་ལ་གདམས་པ), and I read the verse in the root text.
- **Haribhadra's commentary location:** the འགྲེལ་པ་དོན་གསལ། gloss on the ten instructions, in a single long sentence. The part in question reads [ཤའི་དང་། རྣམ་པར་སྨིན་པ་ལས་བྱུང་བའི་ལྷའི་དང་། ཤེས་རབ་དང་ཆོས་དང་སངས་རྒྱས་ཀྱི་སྤྱན་གྲངས་ཇི་ལྟ་བ་བཞིན་དུ་དངོས་པོ་སོ་སོར་ངེས་པ་དང་། སེམས་ཅན་ཐམས་ཅད་ཀྱི་འཆི་འཕོ་བ་དང་། སྐྱེ་བ་དང་།](quote:p407-q05). I read that place in full. Two things stand out. First, Haribhadra puts the qualifier "arisen from maturation" on the divine eye alone among the five. Second, the reason for it shows up a few lines later, in the clairvoyance item, where a second divine eye appears: [སྔོན་གྱི་གནས་རྗེས་སུ་དྲན་པ་དང་། མངོན་པར་འདུ་བྱེད་པ་ལས་བྱུང་བ་ལྷའི་མིག་དང་། ཟག་པ་ཟད་པ་ཤེས་པའི་མངོན་པར་ཤེས་པ](quote:p407-q06), "the divine eye arisen from deliberate cultivation." Haribhadra separates the maturation-born divine eye (one of the five eyes) from the cultivation-born divine eye (one of the six clairvoyances), but he states no definition, draws no boundary on the paths, and says nothing about whether either is a sense consciousness. Every question the yig cha will argue about at p410–p412 is absent from him. He contributes only the object and the qualifier.
- **Text location:** in སྐབས་དང་པོའི་སྤྱི་དོན།, the section heads down to this paragraph run as follows: སྤྱན་ལྔ་ལ་གདམས་པ། (opens p404) → the second of its three parts, བསྟན་བཅོས་ཀྱིས་འགྲེལ་ཚུལ (p405) → ཚིག་དོན་དངོས (p405) → its second division, ༢༽ དོན་གྱི་སྒོ་ནས་གདམས་པ (p405). That last division has five parts. This paragraph is its second. The third of the top-level parts, མཐའ་དཔྱད་པ, begins after the Buddha eye.
- **Section type:** exposition of scripture (ལུང་སྦྱོར): the sūtra is matched to Haribhadra's gloss. There is no ཁ་ཅིག and no reasoning.
- **Argumentative role:** it sets up evidence for the debate that comes later. When p410 denies that every divine eye knows the deaths and births of *all* beings, the words it is qualifying are the ones placed here. Here the phrase is given as Haribhadra's statement of the object. The later debate decides how much of it can go into a definition.

## 4 · Philosophical Exposition & Commentary

**Core meaning.** The sūtra says two things about the bodhisattva's divine eye. First, it knows the divine eye of every class of god from the Four Great Kings to Akaniṣṭha, and none of them knows its own. Second, it knows the deaths and births of beings in countless worlds in all ten directions. Haribhadra reduces this to the object of the second eye: the passing away and the rebirth of all sentient beings. The paragraph records that reduction.

**The Indian background.** Ārya Vimuktisena had already made the second half of the passage the reason for the first: [ཅིའི་ཕྱིར་ཞེ་ན། བྱང་ཆུབ་སེམས་དཔའི་ལྷའི་མིག་གི་ཡུལ་ནི་ཕྱོགས་བཅུའི་སེམས་ཅན་ཐམས་ཅད་ཀྱི་འཆི་འཕོ་བ་དང་སྐྱེ་བ་ཡིན་པའི་ཕྱིར་རོ། །](quote:p407-q07). That is, the gods cannot know the bodhisattva's divine eye because its object is the deaths and births of all beings of the ten directions. Haribhadra takes over Vimuktisena's object. This is not the only Indian reading, though. The Bṛhaṭṭīkā attributed to Vasubandhu describes the divine eye by a different object altogether: [ཏིང་ངེ་འཛིན་དང་སྙོམས་པར་འཇུག་པ་ཐམས་ཅད་ཤེས་པ་ནི་ལྷའི་མིག་གོ། །](quote:p407-q08), "the knowledge of all concentrations and absorptions is the divine eye." These do not contradict each other. Each reads a different half of the same sūtra passage. Haribhadra builds on the death-and-birth half, and that is the one the yig cha takes.

**Je Tsongkhapa.** The ལེགས་བཤད་གསེར་ཕྲེང explains Haribhadra's pair and his qualifier together: [དགེ་བའི་ལས་ཀྱི་རྣམ་སྨིན་ཙམ་ལས་བྱུང་བའི་ལྷའི་སྤྱན་ནི། ཕྱོགས་བཅུའི་སེམས་ཅན་ཐམས་ཅད་ཀྱི་འདི་ནས་གཞན་དུ་འཆི་འཕོ་བ་དང་གཞན་ནས་འདིར་སྐྱེ་བ་མཐོང་བའི་ཡུལ་ཅན་ཏེ།](quote:p407-q09). In translation: "the divine eye that arises solely from the maturation of virtuous karma is the subject that sees the dying of all beings of the ten directions from here to elsewhere and their being born from elsewhere to here." The word ཙམ, "solely," is Tsongkhapa's addition. It sharpens Haribhadra's line against the cultivated divine eye of the clairvoyances. He then explains the god-realms clause, which is the part this paragraph actually quotes, by way of the Bṛhaṭṭīkā: [ལྷ་རྣམས་ཀྱི་མིག་བྱང་སེམས་ཀྱི་ལྷའི་མིག་གིས་ཤེས་པར་གསུངས་པ་ནི་དེ་དག་གི་མིག་དང་དེའི་ཡུལ་དང་ལྷ་དེ་དག་གི་བསམ་གཏན་དང་ཏིང་ངེ་འཛིན་རྣམས་ཤེས་ལ།](quote:p407-q10). To "know the gods' divine eye" means to know their eyes, the objects of those eyes, and their dhyānas and samādhis. So Tsongkhapa brings both Indian readings together at the point where this paragraph cuts the sūtra.

**Gyaltsab Je.** The རྣམ་བཤད་སྙིང་པོའི་རྒྱན། gives the same gloss in slightly different words and specifies that the karma is *contaminated*: [སྔོན་དགེ་བ་ཟག་བཅས་ཀྱི་ལས་ཀྱི་རྣམ་པར་སྨིན་པ་ལས་བྱུང་པའི་ལྷའི་སྤྱན་ནི་སེམས་ཅན་ཐམས་ཅད་ཀྱི་འཆི་འཕོ་དང་སྐྱེ་བ་ཇི་ལྟ་བ་བཞིན་དུ་ཤེས་པ་དང་།](quote:p407-q11). He then adds what neither Haribhadra nor the sūtra gives, which is the path boundaries: [ཤའི་སྤྱན་ཚོགས་ལམ་ནས་དང་། ལྷའི་སྤྱན་སྦྱོར་ལམ་དང་། ཤེས་རབ་ཀྱི་སྤྱན་མཐོང་ལམ་དང་།](quote:p407-q12). The divine eye comes from the path of preparation. The yig cha is commenting on Gyaltsab's text, and it quotes this line at p412.

**Jetsun Chökyi Gyaltsen's own view.** In this paragraph he quotes Haribhadra's phrase without comment. A few paragraphs later, in the same text, he refuses to let the phrase become a pervasion: [ལྷའི་སྤྱན་ལ་ཡང་སེམས་ཅན་ཐམས་ཅད་ཀྱི་འཆི་འཕོ་བ་སོགས་མངོན་སུམ་དུ་རྟོགས་པས་མ་ཁྱབ་སྟེ། དེ་ལ་ནུས་མཐུ་ཆེ་ཆུང་གི་ཁྱད་པར་ཡོད་པའི་ཕྱིར།](quote:p407-q13). In translation: "the divine eye is not pervaded by directly realising the deaths and so on of all sentient beings, because it has differences of greater and lesser power." His own definition in the སྐབས་དང་པོའི་མཐའ་དཔྱོད། keeps Haribhadra's object and quietly drops one word: [དེ་གང་ཞིག རང་གི་བདག་རྐྱེན་དུ་གྱུར་པའི་ལྷའི་མིག་ལ་བརྟེན་ནས་བྱུང་བའི་སེམས་ཅན་གྱི་འཆི་འཕོ་བ་དང་སྐྱེ་བ་མངོན་སུམ་དུ་རྟོགས་པ་དེ། ལྷའི་སྤྱན་གྱི་མཚན་ཉིད།](quote:p407-q14). It speaks of "the deaths and births of *sentient beings*," not of *all* sentient beings. The ཐམས་ཅད that this paragraph quotes from Haribhadra is exactly what the definition removes. That is the most useful thing to know when reading this paragraph. Haribhadra's words are quoted in full here because they state what the sūtra describes, which is the bodhisattva's divine eye at its fullest extent. They do not give the minimum that makes something a divine eye.

**Sera Jey specificity.** The paragraph itself is ordinary exposition, and every Gelug commentary glosses the phrase the same way. What is distinctive is how the text is laid out. Chökyi Gyaltsen gives the Haribhadra phrase in full and in its own place, then takes it apart only when he reaches the analysis section. A student who memorises the ཚིག་དོན section therefore has the exact words that the later ཁ་ཅིག will misuse as a definition.

## 5 · Bones of Contention 🦴

The paragraph disputes nothing on its surface. But the phrase it isolates is the object of three live disputes, which all come up from p410 on, and the paragraph is where their proof-text is fixed.

**1. Is "knowing the deaths and births of all beings" a pervasion of the divine eye?**

- **The opponent (ཁ་ཅིག, reported in the mtha' dpyod and at p410):** yes. He defines the divine eye as directly realising the deaths and births of all beings, taking Haribhadra's words at face value.
- **Chökyi Gyaltsen:** no, because divine eyes differ in power ([q13](quote:p407-q13)). His definition drops "all" ([q14](quote:p407-q14)).
- **Jamyang Zhepa (Gomang):** also no, on a different ground. The sūtra is describing the *bodhisattva's* divine eye and states only its limits: [འདོད་མི་ནུས་ཏེ། འདི་བྱང་ཆུབ་སེམས་དཔའི་ལྷའི་སྤྱན་བཤད་པའི་སྐབས་ཡིན་པས་མ་མཐའ་དང་ཡ་མཐའ་བཟུང་བ་ཙམ་ཡིན་པའི་ཕྱིར།](quote:p407-q17). He supports this with the Bṛhaṭṭīkā, the same passage quoted above ([q08](quote:p407-q08)). The Sera Jey and Gomang textbooks agree on the conclusion. Chökyi Gyaltsen argues from the nature of the eye (it has grades). Jamyang Zhepa argues from the rhetoric of the sūtra (it marks a range).

**2. From which path is the divine eye obtained?**

- **Gyaltsab Je:** from the path of preparation ([q12](quote:p407-q12)).
- **Chökyi Gyaltsen:** he accepts the line but denies that it is a strict boundary. A bodhisattva on the path of accumulation who has the fourth dhyāna as support already has the divine eye: [བསམ་གཏན་བཞི་པའི་རྟེན་ཅན་གྱི་བྱང་སེམས་ཚོགས་ལམ་པའི་རྒྱུད་ལ་ལྷའི་སྤྱན་ཡོད་པའི་ཕྱིར།](quote:p407-q15). At p412 he reads Gyaltsab as speaking of the principal case (གཙོ་ཆེ་བ), not as a pervasion, and he cites the gser phreng for the view that there is no fault in obtaining it on the path of accumulation. In this text there is no quarrel with Gyaltsab. He reinterprets Gyaltsab so that the two agree. I did not find the gser phreng sentence cited at p412 by searching the gser phreng file with a short phrase. Its wording is therefore taken on Chökyi Gyaltsen's report and not checked here.

**3. Is the divine eye a sense consciousness?**

- **The opponent reported in the mtha' dpyod:** defines the divine eye as a cognition based on the physical eye faculty of one who has attained the fourth dhyāna. That would make it a sense consciousness.
- **Chökyi Gyaltsen:** rejects this. On that definition a bodhisattva's divine eye on the path of preparation would be a sense consciousness, and so would the clairvoyance of the divine eye. His own definition still speaks of arising "in dependence on the divine eye as its own dominant condition." The passages I read do not settle how he reconciles this with denying that it is a sense consciousness, so I leave that open.
- **Panchen Sonam Drakpa (Loseling):** says outright that Gyaltsab Je's own wording on this point is provisional: [སྐབས་འདིར་རྣམ་བཤད་ལས་ལྷའི་མིག་གཟུགས་ཅན་དང་བ་དང་། དེ་ལ་བརྟེན་པའི་མངོན་ཤེས་དབང་ཤེས་སུ་གསུངས་པ་དྲང་དོན་དུ་འགྲེལ་དགོས་པར་སེམས་ཏེ།](quote:p407-q16). He goes on to say that all five eyes and six clairvoyances must be mental consciousness. I found Panchen's description of the རྣམ་བཤད་ statement; I did not locate the sentence in Gyaltsab Je's text myself.

**Why does it matter?** Haribhadra attached the qualifier "arisen from maturation" to this eye alone, and it is the only thread linking the five eyes to the six clairvoyances. If the divine eye of the five eyes knew *all* deaths and births just by being what it is, it would outrank the cultivated clairvoyance of the same name. The graded eye that the yig cha argues for keeps the two in proportion. It also leaves the bodhisattva's unlimited divine eye, which is what the sūtra describes, as the top of a range and not the entry condition.

**Resolution.** Sera Jey takes Haribhadra's phrase as a true description of the object of the bodhisattva's divine eye at its fullest, which is how this paragraph presents it. It does not take the phrase as a definition. The definition keeps the object and drops "all," and Gyaltsab's path boundaries are read as describing the typical case.

## 6 · Key Terminology & Technical Concepts

- **ལྷའི་སྤྱན། / ལྷའི་མིག** — the divine eye.
  - **Sanskrit equivalent:** *divya-cakṣus*.
  - **How understood here:** the second of the five eyes. The sūtra uses མིག throughout, while Haribhadra and the yig cha use the honorific སྤྱན for the eye as one of the bodhisattva's qualities. Panchen Sonam Drakpa takes the two as synonymous. Chökyi Gyaltsen's definition keeps them apart: the ལྷའི་མིག is the dominant condition (བདག་རྐྱེན), and the ལྷའི་སྤྱན is the realisation that arises in dependence on it.
  - **Debates:** whether it is sense or mental consciousness (§5.3), and whether its range is fixed (§5.1).

- **རྣམ་པར་སྨིན་པ་ལས་བྱུང་བ། vs. མངོན་པར་འདུ་བྱེད་པ་ལས་བྱུང་བ།** — arisen from maturation vs. arisen from deliberate cultivation.
  - **How understood here:** Haribhadra's line between the divine eye of the five eyes and the divine-eye clairvoyance ([q05](quote:p407-q05), [q06](quote:p407-q06)). Tsongkhapa writes "from the maturation of virtuous karma *alone*." Gyaltsab specifies *contaminated* virtue from earlier lives. Panchen Sonam Drakpa puts the contrast as earlier karma against dhyāna cultivated in this life.
  - **Debates:** the yig cha does not raise it in this paragraph. It matters because the power-gradation argument of p410 only works if the maturation-born eye can differ from person to person.

- **འཆི་འཕོ་བ་དང་སྐྱེ་བ།** — death-transference and birth.
  - **Sanskrit equivalent:** *cyuty-upapāda*.
  - **How understood here:** Tsongkhapa spells out the pair as passing from here to elsewhere and being born from elsewhere to here ([q09](quote:p407-q09)). It is the object of the second eye as Haribhadra assigns it, and the same pair names the Buddha's power of knowing death and birth. Here it is the bodhisattva's version, bounded by the realms the eye can see.

- **ཡོངས་སུ་དག་པ།** — completely pure.
  - **How understood here:** the sūtra's word for each of the five eyes as a bodhisattva has it. It is what makes this passage a description of the bodhisattva's eye at full development, and it underlies Jamyang Zhepa's reading that the sūtra only marks limits.
