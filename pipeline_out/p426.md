# The printers' colophon — who wrote the dedication verse, and for what

## 1 · Passage & Context

**Source Text:**

> ཅེས་འཛམ་གླིང་ཡངས་པའི་ཁྱོན་ལ་འཚེ་མེད་ཞི་བའི་ལམ་སྟོན་རྣམ་འགྲེལ་ཤཱཀྱ་སེངྒེའི་རིང་ལུགས་སྒོ་ཀུན་ནས་རྒྱས་པ་སོགས་འཕྲལ་ཡུན་ལེགས་ཚོགས་དུ་མའི་ཆེད་དུ་དམིགས། ནང་བསྟན་གྲུབ་མཐའ་རིས་མེད་ཀྱི་གསུང་རབ་དག་དཔར་དུ་བསྐྲུན་དང་བསྐྲུན་འགྱུར་གང་ལའང་སྦྱར་ཆོག་པའི་དགེ་བསྔོའི་སྨོན་ཚིག་ཏུ་ཤཱཀྱའི་དགེ་སྦྱོང་ཆོས་སྨྲ་བ་ངག་དབང་བློ་བཟང་བསྟན་འཛིན་རྒྱ་མཚོས་སྦྱར་བའོ།། །། དགེའོ།།

**Preceding Section:** p425, headed པར་བྱང་སྨོན་ཚིག ("printing colophon and prayer"), is an eight-line verse. It calls "this excellent explanation" (ལེགས་བཤད་བདུད་རྩི་འདི) a slow-flowing river of the boundless modes of dharma that tends, with one taste, toward the ocean of liberation and peace, and whose ripples of words wash away the stains of the three poisons. By the force of the white virtue of having printed it (དཔར་དུ་བསྐྲུན་པའི་རྣམ་དཀར་ཕུང་པོའི་མཐུས), it prays that the hundred thousand doors of the true dharma may be thrown open and that all beings of the five realms may gain command of the treasury of definite goodness (ངེས་ལེགས). Nothing in that verse is specific to the *Abhisamayālaṃkāra* or to this commentary. Before p425 the text itself (p424) had ended on Haribhadra's summary of the ten instructions — ཞེས་པའི་གཞུང་འདི་རྣམས་གསུངས་སོ — with no closing formula of Chökyi Gyaltsen's or his scribe's in between.

**Following Section:** None. p426 is the last paragraph of the text. In the source file what follows is the edition's apparatus — sigla for three collated witnesses (ག, ཏ, ཀ) with folio and line numbers, and references to the Kangyur and Tengyur *dpe bsdur ma* — which is not part of the paragraph set.

**What this paragraph is doing.** It is the prose tail of the printers' colophon. The ཅེས at its head closes p425's verse; the paragraph then says what that verse was composed for, that it was written to be attachable to any printing of Buddhist scripture of any tenet system, and who wrote it — ཤཱཀྱའི་དགེ་སྦྱོང་ཆོས་སྨྲ་བ་ངག་དབང་བློ་བཟང་བསྟན་འཛིན་རྒྱ་མཚོ, the ordination name of the 14th Dalai Lama. It belongs to the publication, not to Chökyi Gyaltsen's work, and it makes no philosophical claim.

**On རྣམ་འགྲེལ.** The paragraph reads ལམ་སྟོན་རྣམ་འགྲེལ་ཤཱཀྱ་སེངྒེ. Read literally རྣམ་འགྲེལ is "commentary", which does not stand in apposition to the Buddha. The same prose, word for word, stands at the end of thirteen works in this corpus, and nine of them read རྣམ་འདྲེན ("guide", an epithet of the Buddha) at this point: the printing of the root text, [ཤཱཀྱ་སེངྒེའི་རིང་ལུགས](quote:p426-q06), and those of Haribhadra's འགྲེལ་པ་དོན་གསལ།, both halves of Tsongkhapa's ལེགས་བཤད་གསེར་ཕྲེང་, Gyaltsab Je's རྣམ་བཤད་སྙིང་པོའི་རྒྱན།, Khedrub Je's རྟོགས་དཀའི་སྣང་བ།, Chökyi Gyaltsen's དགེ་འདུན་ཉི་ཤུའི་མཐའ་གཅོད། and སྐབས་བཞི་པའི་སྤྱི་དོན།, and Delek Nyima's biography of Chökyi Gyaltsen. The long recension in the Sungbum also has [རྣམ་འདྲེན](quote:p426-q03). Four read རྣམ་འགྲེལ: this volume, the first-chapter མཐའ་དཔྱོད།, the fifth-chapter སྤྱི་དོན། and Purbuchok's བསྡུས་གྲྭ་རིགས་ལམ་ཆུང་ངུ་།. The translation above follows the sense of the majority reading. The tally is a report of the witnesses, not an emendation.

**On སོགས.** "Such as the flourishing … of the Śākya Lion's tradition" (རྒྱས་པ་སོགས) names one aim and gestures at others. The others are nowhere in this paragraph. They are in the long recension, where this clause is the last item of a list. That list starts with an end to [weapons of the atom and every kind of violent harm](quote:p426-q02) and goes on to [relighting the embers of the teaching in Tibet and spreading it in India](quote:p426-q03). The abrupt ཅེས་འཛམ་གླིང that opens p426 is where the middle recension starts, after those clauses were cut.

## 3 · Structural Context (Dkar Chag)

**Abhisamayālaṃkāra location:** None, and the pack names no lemma. Nothing in the root verses corresponds. The only relationship to the Ornament is that the same colophon was bound into the root text's own printing. There it comes after the translators' colophon (པཎྚིཏ་གོ་མི་འཆི་མེད་དང་། ལོ་ཙཱ་བ་བློ་ལྡན་ཤེས་རབ་ཀྱི་འགྱུར།), which is to say outside the work: [the printing of the Ornament](quote:p426-q06) carries the identical prose.

**Haribhadra's commentary location:** I read the aligned place as the probe requires. The last thing the yig cha quotes before its colophon is Haribhadra's summary of the ten instructions, [འདི་ཙམ་ཁོ་ནས་དོན་ཐམས་ཅད་ཕུན་སུམ་ཚོགས་པར་འགྱུར་བས་གདམས་ངག་ནི་རྣམ་པ་བཅུའོ།](quote:p426-q08). In འགྲེལ་པ་དོན་གསལ། that sentence closes nothing: the next words are the heading ༼དགེ་འདུན་ཉི་ཤུ། ༽ and Haribhadra goes straight on to the twenty saṅgha. So the break at which this volume ends is the yig cha's division of chapter one into separate volumes (the twenty saṅgha have their own དགེ་འདུན་ཉི་ཤུའི་མཐའ་གཅོད།), not Haribhadra's. Haribhadra's own colophon, at the end of the whole commentary, is a different kind of text. It is an author's dedication of the merit of composing, [དགེ་བ་བདག་གིས་གང་ཐོབ་པ། ། དེ་ཡིས་སྐྱེ་བོ་ཐམས་ཅད་ནི། ། བདེ་གཤེགས་ཤེས་རབ་ཐོབ་པར་ཤོག](quote:p426-q07), and it is followed by the མཛད་བྱང and the འགྱུར་བྱང. In that edition the printers' colophon comes after all three. p425–p426 corresponds to that last, publisher's layer and to nothing Haribhadra wrote.

**Text location:** The pack's outline puts པར་བྱང་སྨོན་ཚིག (opening at p425, page 322) under སྒོམ་ལམ་ལ་གདམས་པ། (opening at p423, page 318). That nesting only records where the heading happens to fall. The printers' colophon is not a subdivision of the instruction on the path of meditation, and the text's own sa bcad has no place for it. I found no closing formula of the author's in this volume. The companion first-chapter མཐའ་དཔྱོད། has one — [རྗེ་བཙུན་ཆོས་ཀྱི་རྒྱལ་མཚན་གྱི་གསུང་ཟིན་བྲིས་སུ་བཀོད་པ … སྐབས་དང་པོའི་མཐའ་དཔྱོད་བཤད་ཟིན་ཏོ།](quote:p426-q09) — followed by a dedicatory verse before the printers take over. In the སྤྱི་དོན། as this file has it, Haribhadra's words at the end of p424 lead straight into the heading of p425. Whether the source print omitted a closing formula or never had one, I cannot tell from this file.

**Section type:** A publisher's colophon (པར་བྱང), prose part: purpose, scope and authorship of the preceding dedication verse.

**Argumentative role:** None. It advances no thesis and refutes no one. Its only textual function is to attribute p425.

## 4 · Philosophical Exposition & Commentary

**Core meaning.** The paragraph makes three claims about the verse before it. First, its purpose is the "immediate and long-term" good of the world, headed by the spread of the Buddha's tradition as a path without harm. The pair འཕྲལ་ཡུན picks up the verse's closing prayer for ངེས་ལེགས (definite goodness), whose usual partner is temporary higher rebirth. Second, its scope is generic by design: it is a བསྔོ་བ written to be *transferable*, attachable to the printing of any Buddhist scripture of any school (གྲུབ་མཐའ་རིས་མེད), past or future. That is why the verse says only "this excellent explanation" and never names a text. Third, its author is the monk who signs himself ཤཱཀྱའི་དགེ་སྦྱོང་ཆོས་སྨྲ་བ་ངག་དབང་བློ་བཟང་བསྟན་འཛིན་རྒྱ་མཚོ.

**Who the signatory is, and on what evidence.** The name is the ordination name of the 14th Dalai Lama, and the style — Śākya renunciant, speaker of dharma — is his usual colophon signature. p426 itself gives no further mark. The long recension of the same prose, printed with Geshe Thubten Rinchen's edition of the *Pramāṇavārttika* commentary, does: it puts the honorific ༧ before the signature, [དགེ་བསྔོའི་སྨོན་ཚིག་ཏུ ༧་ཤཱཀྱའི་དགེ་སྦྱོང་ཆོས་སྨྲ་བ་ངག་དབང་བློ་བཟང་བསྟན་འཛིན་རྒྱ་མཚོས་སྦྱར་བའོ།](quote:p426-q04). The identification therefore rests on the name, the formula and that mark. None of the thirteen commentary printings says it in words.

**The occasion the short form hides.** Read on its own, p426's "harmless path of peace" sounds like a conventional epithet. The long recension shows it is pointed. It opens by saying that [the precious teaching, which blazed in the Land of Snows like daylight, has been reduced to a mere name by the barbarians' unbearable ruinous conduct](quote:p426-q01). It then prays for [an end to weapons of the atom and every kind of violent harm spreading day by day](quote:p426-q02), and for [the embers of the teaching to be relit in Tibet and to spread in India, the land of the Āryas](quote:p426-q03). Only after that does it reach p426's clause. The colophon is an exile-period text, and the reprinting of the monastic curriculum in India is part of what it dedicates. The version printed here has been cut to leave out the political clauses. I did not find in the corpus who made the cut, or when.

**Three recensions.** The corpus shows the same prose at three lengths:

- **Short:** ཅེས goes straight to ནང་བསྟན་གྲུབ་མཐའ་རིས་མེད with no statement of aims. It appears in a sister volume of this series, [the second-chapter སྤྱི་དོན།](quote:p426-q05), and in five other volumes of Chökyi Gyaltsen: the second-chapter མཐའ་དཔྱོད།, and the སྤྱི་དོན on Madhyamaka, on drang nges, on the Abhidharmakośa and on Vinaya.
- **Middle:** the form of p426. It adds only the clause about the Śākya Lion's tradition spreading across the world.
- **Long:** the Sungbum form with the Tibet, India and atomic-weapons clauses.

p426's middle form is shared by the Ornament, Haribhadra, Tsongkhapa, Gyaltsab Je, Khedrub Je and several of Chökyi Gyaltsen's volumes. That the two chapter-one volumes carry the middle form and the chapter-two volumes the short one suggests they were set at different times. That is an inference from the witnesses, not something any colophon states.

**Historical debate.** There is none about the content. The commentators the rubric names — Chökyi Gyaltsen, Panchen Sonam Drakpa, Tsongkhapa, Gyaltsab Je — lived centuries before this colophon and cannot be said to read it. The one comparison the tradition supports is structural. An Indian śāstra ends with an author's dedication of the merit of composing (Haribhadra, above). A Gelug yig cha adds its author's or scribe's closing formula and a prayer for the lineage (the first-chapter མཐའ་དཔྱོད།, above). A modern printing adds a third layer that dedicates the merit of *printing*, and p425–p426 is that layer.

**Sera Jey specificity.** Not distinctive. The same colophon closes Gelug works of every house in this corpus: the Tsongkhapa and his-disciples volumes, Chökyi Gyaltsen's Sera Jey textbooks, Purbuchok's Collected Topics, and a biography of Chökyi Gyaltsen. It was written expressly to be used by any school ("རིས་མེད"). I found nothing in the corpus that names the printing house of this edition, so I cannot say from the evidence whether it was a Sera Jey printing.

## 5 · Bones of Contention 🦴

**What is disputed?** Nothing doctrinal. The paragraph does not open with ཁ་ཅིག, states no position and refutes none. That is simply true of a printers' colophon. What is open is textual and attributional.

**Who argues what?** Nobody argues these points in the texts. The witnesses disagree with one another:

- **རྣམ་འགྲེལ against རྣམ་འདྲེན.** Four printings (this volume, the first-chapter མཐའ་དཔྱོད།, the fifth-chapter སྤྱི་དོན།, Purbuchok) read རྣམ་འགྲེལ. Nine, together with the long recension, read རྣམ་འདྲེན. A reader of this volume who takes རྣམ་འགྲེལ at face value gets "the commentary Śākya Lion", which is not sense. The translation follows the majority, and this note marks the choice.
- **How much of the colophon is present.** Short, middle and long recensions coexist even within one textbook series. Anyone who cites "the colophon of the སྐབས་དང་པོའི་སྤྱི་དོན།" should know that its wording is not the same as that of the second-chapter volume beside it.
- **Where it sits.** The outline nests p425–p426 under སྒོམ་ལམ་ལ་གདམས་པ།. That nesting is mechanical. The passage is not part of that topic and not part of Chökyi Gyaltsen's work.
- **Who the author is.** The naming as the 14th Dalai Lama is secure from the name and the signature formula, and the long recension's ༧ confirms it. p426 by itself carries no title or mark.

**Why does it matter?** A reader who treats p425–p426 as Chökyi Gyaltsen's own closing verses would attribute to a sixteenth-century author a twentieth-century dedication, one written, as its long form says, for a teaching in exile. And a reader quoting this volume's colophon for its wording will quote an unconstruable རྣམ་འགྲེལ unless warned.

**Resolution.** No Sera Jey doctrinal position is at stake. The sound reading is: p425–p426 is a publisher's colophon by the 14th Dalai Lama, written to be transferable and reproduced across this whole print series. It is not part of the commentary, and it gives no evidence about Chökyi Gyaltsen's views.

## 6 · Key Terminology & Technical Concepts

- **པར་བྱང་ (printing colophon):** the colophon of a printed edition, as distinct from the author's མཛད་བྱང and the translators' འགྱུར་བྱང
  - **How understood here:** it heads p425 and governs p426; it is the outermost layer of the book, after whatever the author and translators wrote
  - **Debates:** none; but its placement inside the outline here is an artefact of segmentation

- **དགེ་བསྔོ / བསྔོ་བ (dedication of virtue):** directing the merit of an act to an end
  - **Sanskrit equivalent:** pariṇāmanā
  - **How understood here:** the act dedicated is the printing (དཔར་དུ་བསྐྲུན་པ), not the composition. Contrast Haribhadra's author's dedication, which offers the merit of writing the commentary.

- **བསྐྲུན་དང་བསྐྲུན་འགྱུར་གང་ལའང་སྦྱར་ཆོག་པ (attachable to any printing, done or to be done):** the colophon declares itself a reusable formula
  - **How understood here:** this is why the verse in p425 names no text, and why the same words close the Ornament, Haribhadra, Tsongkhapa and Chökyi Gyaltsen alike

- **གྲུབ་མཐའ་རིས་མེད (without partiality among tenet systems):** of every Buddhist school, without distinction
  - **How understood here:** the scope of the dedication is every Buddhist scripture, stated in a Gelug monastic textbook; it is a statement about the printing programme, not a doxographical claim

- **འཚེ་མེད་ཞི་བའི་ལམ (the harmless path of peace):** the Buddha's path characterised by non-harm
  - **Sanskrit equivalent:** ahiṃsā for འཚེ་མེད
  - **How understood here:** the long recension sets it explicitly against the weapons and violence of the present age, so the epithet has a pointed contemporary reference

- **འཕྲལ་ཡུན (immediate and long-term):** the pair of temporary and ultimate benefit
  - **How understood here:** it matches the verse's prayer for ངེས་ལེགས, definite goodness, whose standard partner is མངོན་མཐོ, higher rebirth

- **ཤཱཀྱའི་དགེ་སྦྱོང་ཆོས་སྨྲ་བ (Śākya renunciant, speaker of dharma):** the self-designation of the signatory
  - **Sanskrit equivalent:** śramaṇa for དགེ་སྦྱོང; dharmabhāṇaka for ཆོས་སྨྲ་བ
  - **How understood here:** a humble monastic signature, used here in place of any title; the identification of the signer rests on the name that follows it
